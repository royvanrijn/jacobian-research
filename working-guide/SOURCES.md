# Sources and rationale

These sources informed the guide. They document particular failures and provide expert guidance; they do not measure a consensus among “most people.” Source notes were checked on 17 November 2026. Empirical studies, position papers, teaching advice, and publication policies support different kinds of conclusions.

The operational rules in this directory are this repository's working policy. In particular, keeping ongoing research and internal repository details out of paper prose implements the requested publication standard. The exposition sources below help explain why a direct, self-contained presentation serves a reviewer. Necessary mathematical qualifications, required disclosures, and stable artifact citations remain appropriate.

## Citation reliability

William H. Walters and Esther Isabelle Wilder, [Fabrication and errors in the bibliographic citations generated by ChatGPT](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-41032-5), *Scientific Reports* 13, 14045 (2023).

This empirical study audited references in generated literature reviews and found fabricated entries as well as errors in references to real works. It directly motivates checking bibliographic identity. The guide additionally requires checking the cited passage against the claim it is meant to support. The study's historical model results do not establish error rates for current models, all AI-assisted writing, or published mathematical papers.

**Guide application:** [citation checks](PAPERS.md#verify-citations-and-attribution) and the [reference evidence record](TEMPLATES.md#reference-evidence-record).

## Argument and reviewer burden

Jianghao Lin and coauthors, [Stop DDoS Attacking the Research Community with AI-Generated Survey Papers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09686), submitted 9 October 2025; [version 1 text](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.09686v1).

This position paper criticizes redundant surveys, shallow synthesis, attribution problems, factual errors, and demands on readers and reviewers. It supports requiring an identifiable contribution and an organized synthesis rather than a topic list. It is an argument focused on surveys, not a representative opinion poll or a test of individual authorship.

Yerim Oh, Young-Jun Lee, Jaewoo Ahn, Gunhee Kim, and Dongyeop Kang, [Science or Slop?: Benchmarking and Mitigating Scientific Slop in AI-Generated Papers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00531), version 1 submitted 30 September 2026; [version 1 text](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.00531v1).

This preprint studies failures across structure, argument, and artifacts in matched AI-generated and human-written papers. It draws attention to repetition, poorly connected reasoning, weak claim support, and unhelpful artifacts. The sample is mainly computer science. Its proposed measures are research instruments, not mathematical correctness certificates or universal writing rules.

**Guide application:** [recurring failures](PAPERS.md#recognize-recurring-failures-in-ai-assisted-papers), cross-section consistency checks, and requiring figures and examples to do explanatory work. The rule is to repair the argument rather than optimize a detection score.

## Mathematical practice

Terence Tao, [Mathematics in the age of AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16753), 2026; [version 1 text](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.16753v1), especially Sections 6–8.

This expert essay discusses mathematical understanding, verification, attribution, and review under increased production of AI-assisted proofs. Its concerns include excessive treatment of routine steps while key ideas and context remain hard to find. Parts of the essay reason conditionally about future capabilities. It supplies a mathematical perspective, not a controlled prevalence study or a claim that all AI-assisted proofs have these faults.

**Guide application:** expose the proof's main mechanism, concentrate detail on difficult inferences, and distinguish formal checking, internal review, and human understanding in [Mathematical validity](MATHEMATICS.md).

## Exposition guidance

Donald E. Knuth, Tracy Larrabee, and Paul M. Roberts, *Mathematical Writing*, MAA (1989), based on a Stanford course in 1987. [Knuth's author page](https://cs.stanford.edu/~knuth/klr.html); [notes hosted by JMLR](https://jmlr.csail.mit.edu/reviewing-papers/knuth_mathematical_writing.pdf), especially Section 1.

The notes discuss mathematical prose, equations within sentences, notation, and the reader's path through an argument. They motivate consistent symbols and readable prose around displays. These are teaching notes with examples and varied opinions; stylistic suggestions need judgment.

Martin Schweizer, [How does one write a mathematical text or paper?](https://people.math.ethz.ch/~mschweiz/Instructions-for-writing.pdf), dated 12 January 2023.

This mathematical teaching guide stresses a linear exposition, defined notation, explicit assumptions and conclusions, rigorous proof or precise references, and a readable introduction. It also explains the balance between missing steps and excessive detail. It is advice written for student mathematical texts, useful beyond that setting without dictating every research article's format.

Simon Peyton Jones, [How to Write a Great Research Paper](https://simon.peytonjones.org/great-research-paper/), author-maintained teaching resource; [linked slides](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2016/07/How-to-write-a-great-research-paper.pdf), especially slides 18–23 and 36–38.

The advice emphasizes a central contribution, examples, support for introduction claims, and a direct explanation of the idea rather than a discovery diary. It motivates a reader-oriented outline. It comes from computer science; suggested lengths and ordering are not universal requirements for mathematics papers.

**Guide application:** [paper structure and proof flow](PAPERS.md), the [adaptable outline](TEMPLATES.md#paper-outline), and a review that checks what an unfamiliar reader can follow.

## Responsibility and disclosure

International Committee of Medical Journal Editors, [Use of AI by Authors](https://icmje.org/recommendations/browse/artificial-intelligence/ai-use-by-authors.html), live recommendations checked 17 November 2026.

This official policy assigns responsibility to human authors, calls for disclosure of AI assistance, and requires review for inaccuracies and attribution problems. It concerns medical journals and does not bind a mathematics venue. Use the actual target's current instructions for submission details.

**Guide application:** truthful assistance statements and checking current target requirements in [the release checklist](CHECKLIST.md#submission-or-deposit). Neither removing AI-related phrasing nor inserting a generic disclosure substitutes for checking the mathematics.

## Applying the evidence responsibly

Several independent sources point toward checking sources, exposing the central argument, removing repetition, and respecting reviewer effort. That convergence motivates the guide's checks. It does not show that a particular writing style proves AI use, that all AI assistance lowers quality, or that a passing editorial checklist proves a theorem.

When updating these notes, inspect the underlying source, preserve its scope and version, and revise only the rules its evidence actually affects. Check current submission policies at the time of submission. Keep source review history in Git rather than accumulating dated copies of this guide.
