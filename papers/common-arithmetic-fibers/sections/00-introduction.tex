\section{Introduction}

A polynomial map with nonzero constant Jacobian is \'etale.  Its fibers are
therefore finite \'etale schemes, possibly empty.  This raises a simple reverse
question: given a finite \'etale algebra $A/K$, can we build a Keller map with
$\Spec A$ as one of its fibers?

There is one important word in that question.  The fiber should be \emph{full}:
its length must equal the geometric degree of the map.  Otherwise some generic
inverse sheets may have escaped to infinity, and the special fiber records
only part of the inverse problem.  Our main result answers this prescribed
algebra question: every finite \'etale $K$-algebra of rank at least three occurs
as a full fiber of an explicit Keller map in three variables.  The statement
about possible ranks---all nonzero ranks except two---is a consequence, not the
main content of the theorem.

For rank $N\geq3$, the construction starts from a squarefree presentation
\[
 A\simeq K[T]/(P).
\]
Choose $a\in K$ with $P'(a)P'''(a)\neq0$ and set
\[
 G(S)=P(a+S)-P(a).
\]
The polynomial $G$ is inserted into one explicit three-variable
quadratic-gauge map.  A fixed linear change in the target makes its Jacobian
one.  The resulting coordinate degrees are bounded by $6N+2$.

The family begins with the announced counterexample itself.  Substituting
\[
 G(S)=S+S^3
\]
recovers Alp\"oge's three coordinates exactly, with no conjugation or output
rescaling.  The higher-degree construction is therefore an extension of the
announced map rather than a separate template that happens to share its
Jacobian.

The displayed map is fairly large, but its inverse problem is deliberately
small.  It is organized around the marked coordinate
\[
 S=\frac{x}{1+xy},
\]
and reduces to a single polynomial equation
\[
 E_{\Pi,B,C}(S)=0.
\]
At an arbitrary target with nonzero first coordinate, the whole fiber is
\[
 \operatorname{Spec}\left((K[S]/(E))[1/\overline{E'}]\right).
\]
This is the natural answer even when $E$ has repeated factors: the source map
is \'etale, so only roots at which $E'$ is invertible can occur.  At the target
used for realization, $E=P(a+S)$ is squarefree, hence $E'$ is already a unit
modulo $E$ and the localization disappears.

The reconstruction is stronger than a count of geometric points.  Its formulas
are natural in the test algebra, so they identify the entire fiber functor,
including nilpotents and every scalar extension.  After translating back from
$S$ to $T$, the literal fiber is the original algebra $K[T]/(P)$.  Nothing
arithmetic has been replaced by a numerical proxy.

The proof splits into four steps.  First, a coefficientwise calculation builds
the all-degree map and proves its constant Jacobian and degree bound.  Second,
the root-to-source formulas prove the localized-fiber theorem and, in the
squarefree case, identify the quotient $K[S]/(E)$ with the whole scheme fiber.
Third, translation turns the inverse polynomial into the prescribed $P$.
Finally, monogenicity handles arbitrary finite \'etale algebras, while the
Campbell--Razar--Wright theorem rules out geometric degree two.

Once the realization theorem is proved, the arithmetic applications are
immediate.  An irreducible polynomial gives a connected fiber; a product gives
the same field decomposition; local points, real signature, and Galois action
are inherited from the original algebra.  In particular, the classical
Berend--Bilu quintic produces the smallest possible Hasse-principle failure,
and a second construction places infinitely many such failures inside one
fixed Keller map.

The constructive part is formalized in Lean.  Starting from squarefree $P$ of
degree at least three, Lean chooses an admissible translation, constructs the
actual $\operatorname{MvPolynomial}(\operatorname{Fin}3,K)$ map and target,
proves the Jacobian-one identity and the $6N+2$ bound, and identifies the
literal fiber naturally with maps from $K[T]/(P)$ into every commutative test
algebra.  It also verifies the exact specialization $G(S)=S+S^3$.  The
remaining inputs---monogenicity, the generic irreducibility argument, the
degree-two theorem, and the number-theoretic applications---are kept separate
and proved in the paper rather than presented as formalized results.
