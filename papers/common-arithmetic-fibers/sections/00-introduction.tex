\section{Introduction}

A polynomial map with nonzero constant Jacobian is \'etale.  Its fibers are
therefore finite \'etale schemes, possibly empty.  This raises a simple reverse
question: given a finite \'etale algebra \(A/K\), can we build a Keller map with
\(\Spec A\) as one of its fibers?

There is one important word in that question.  The fiber should be
\emph{full}: its length must equal the geometric degree of the map.  Otherwise
some generic inverse sheets may have escaped to infinity, and the special
fiber records only part of the inverse problem.  Our main result answers the
prescribed-algebra question.  Every finite \'etale \(K\)-algebra of rank at
least three occurs as a full fiber of an explicit Keller map in three
variables.

Together with the elementary fact that every Keller fiber is finite \'etale,
this gives an exact fixed-degree classification.  For \(N\geq3\), the full
fibers of geometric-degree-\(N\) Keller maps in \(\A^3_K\) are precisely the
rank-\(N\) finite \'etale \(K\)-schemes.  The degree-two case is empty by the
Galois case of the Jacobian theorem, while the identity realizes rank one.

For rank \(N\geq3\), the construction starts from a squarefree presentation
\[
 A\simeq K[T]/(P).
\]
Choose \(a\in K\) with \(P'(a)P'''(a)\neq0\) and set
\[
 G(S)=P(a+S)-P(a).
\]
The polynomial \(G\) is inserted into one explicit three-variable
quadratic-gauge map.  A fixed linear change in the target makes its Jacobian
one, and its coordinate degrees are bounded by \(6N+2\).

The family begins with the announced counterexample itself.  The seed
\[
 G(S)=S+S^3
\]
gives Alp\"oge's announced map coordinate for coordinate, with no conjugation
or output rescaling.  The higher-degree construction is therefore an
extension of that map rather than a separate template with the same
Jacobian.

The displayed coordinates are fairly large, but the inverse problem is
deliberately small.  It is organized around
\[
 S=\frac{x}{1+xy}
\]
and reduces to one polynomial equation
\[
 E_{\Pi,B,C}(S)=0.
\]
At a target with nonzero first coordinate, the localized quadratic-gauge
theorem identifies the entire fiber as
\[
 \operatorname{Spec}\left((K[S]/(E))[1/\overline{E'}]\right).
\]
This is the right answer even when \(E\) has repeated factors: the source map
is \'etale, so only roots at which \(E'\) is invertible can occur.  At the
prescribed target, \(E=P(a+S)\) is squarefree, hence \(E'\) is already a unit
modulo \(E\) and the localization disappears.

The same coordinates identify the actual function-field extension.  On the
dense chart \(1+xy\neq0\), the source field is
\[
 K(x,y,z)=K(\Pi,B,S),
\]
and \(S\) has minimal polynomial \(E_{\Pi,B,C}\) over
\(K(\Pi,B,C)\).  Since that polynomial is irreducible of degree \(N\), the
displayed map has geometric degree \(N\).  This comparison is what turns the
literal rank-\(N\) fiber into a full fiber.

The generic inverse problem is also as large as possible in its Galois
theory.  For every admissible degree-\(N\) seed, not just a generic choice of
its coefficients, the geometric and arithmetic Galois groups of
\(E_{\Pi,B,C}\) are both \(S_N\).  The proof fixes \(\Pi\), parametrizes the
repeated-root curve, and shows that all but finitely many vertical slices are
Morse.  The classical Morse-polynomial theorem gives \(S_N\) on one slice,
and a specialization lemma embeds that group into the generic geometric
group.  Over a Hilbertian field, the same fixed map consequently has
infinitely many connected full fibers with splitting-field group \(S_N\).

The reconstruction is stronger than a count of geometric points.  Its
formulas are natural in the test algebra, so they identify the whole fiber
functor, including nilpotents and every scalar extension.  After translating
back from \(S\) to \(T\), the literal fiber is the original algebra
\(K[T]/(P)\).  Nothing arithmetic has been replaced by a numerical proxy.

The proof has five parts.  First, a coefficientwise calculation builds the
all-degree map and proves its constant Jacobian and degree bound.  Second, the
root-to-source formulas give the localized fiber theorem and its squarefree
corollary.  Third, irreducibility and the explicit source-field comparison
give the actual generic degree.  Fourth, the repeated-root parametrization,
the Morse-polynomial theorem, and specialization give full symmetric
monodromy.  Finally, translation prescribes the polynomial quotient, while
monogenicity passes from polynomial quotients to arbitrary finite \'etale
algebras.  The Campbell--Razar--Wright theorem separately excludes geometric
degree two.

The arithmetic applications are then literal transfers.  An irreducible
polynomial gives a connected fiber; a product gives the same field
decomposition; local points, real signature, and Galois action are inherited
from the original algebra.  In particular, the classical Berend--Bilu
quintic produces a rank-five Hasse-principle failure, and a short
Chebotarev argument shows that rank five is minimal over \(\Q\).

The constructive core is formalized in Lean.  Starting from squarefree \(P\)
of degree at least three, Lean chooses an admissible translation, constructs
the actual three-variable polynomial map and target, proves the Jacobian-one
identity and the \(6N+2\) bound, and identifies the literal fiber naturally
with maps from \(K[T]/(P)\) into every commutative test algebra.  It also
proves that the representing quotient is finite \'etale of rank \(N\), proves
the independent-parameter inverse polynomial irreducible of degree \(N\), and
constructs the explicit comparison
\[
 K(x,y,z)\simeq
 K(\Pi,B)(C)[S]/(E_{\Pi,B,C})
\]
over the induced target-field embedding.  Its finrank calculation therefore
machine-checks the actual geometric degree.  The formal development also
checks that the determinant-one normalization is an invertible target
rescaling and exposes one theorem combining determinant, geometric degree,
literal fiber, naturality, finite \'etaleness, rank, and the degree bound.
Monogenicity for arbitrary finite \'etale algebras, symmetric monodromy,
Hilbert irreducibility, the degree-two theorem, and the remaining classical
arithmetic inputs are ordinary proofs.  For the quintic, Lean proves the exact
fiber, rank, finite \'etaleness, absence of rational points, and the existence
of real and three-adic points.
