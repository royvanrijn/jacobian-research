\section{The quadratic gauge}\label{sec:gauge}

The realization theorem rests on one explicit construction.  Let
\begin{equation}
 G(S)=g_1S+g_2S^2+\cdots+g_NS^N\in K[S],
 \qquad g_1g_3g_N\neq0.
 \label{eq:seed}
\end{equation}
In source coordinates $(x,y,z)$ define
\begin{equation}
 t=1+xy,
 \qquad
 q=t^2z+\frac{g_1}{g_3}y^2(1+3t).
 \label{eq:tq}
\end{equation}
The associated map $F_G=(\Pi,B,C):\A^3_K\to\A^3_K$ is
\begin{align}
 \Pi={}&tq,\notag\\
 B={}&y+3\frac{g_3}{g_1}xq+2\frac{g_2}{g_1}tq
       +\sum_{k=4}^N k\frac{g_k}{g_1}
          t^2x^{k-2}q^k,\notag\\
 C={}&x(5-3t)-\frac{g_3}{g_1}x^3z
       -\sum_{k=4}^N(k-2)\frac{g_k}{g_1}(xq)^k.
 \label{eq:gauge-map}
\end{align}
Although the formula is long, it is polynomial and completely mechanical in
the coefficients of $G$.

The point of this particular arrangement appears in the inverse problem.  For
a target $(\Pi,B,C)$ put
\begin{equation}
 G_\Pi(S)=g_1S+\Pi(g_2S^2+g_3S^3)
          +\sum_{k=4}^Ng_k\Pi^kS^k
 \label{eq:Gpi}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
 E_{\Pi,B,C}(S)=G_\Pi(S)-\frac{g_1}{2}(BS^2+C).
 \label{eq:inverse}
\end{equation}
Thus a three-variable inverse problem will reduce to the single equation
$E_{\Pi,B,C}(S)=0$.

For $(\pi,b,c)\in K^*\times K^2$, write $\mathcal F_{\pi,b,c}$ for the fiber
functor
\[
 \mathcal F_{\pi,b,c}(A)
 =\{(x,y,z)\in A^3:F_G(x,y,z)=(\pi,b,c)\}
\]
on commutative $K$-algebras.

\begin{lemma}[Derivative units over test algebras]\label{lem:derivative-unit}
Let $E\in K[S]$ be separable, let $A$ be a commutative $K$-algebra, and let
$s\in A$ satisfy $E(s)=0$.  Then $E'(s)$ is a unit of $A$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Choose $U,V\in K[S]$ with $UE+VE'=1$.  Evaluating at $s$ gives
$V(s)E'(s)=1$.  Since $A$ is commutative, this is a two-sided inverse.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Quadratic-gauge fiber theorem]\label{prop:gauge}
The map $F_G$ satisfies
\[
 \det DF_G=-2.
\]
Its generic inverse equation is $E_{\Pi,B,C}(S)=0$.  Let
$(\pi,b,c)\in K^*\times K^2$.  If $E=E_{\pi,b,c}$ is squarefree of degree
$N$, then for every commutative $K$-algebra $A$ there is a natural bijection
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Hom}_{K\text{-alg}}(K[S]/(E),A)
 \simeq \mathcal F_{\pi,b,c}(A).
 \label{eq:natural-fiber-equivalence}
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\[
 F_G^{-1}(\pi,b,c)\simeq\Spec K[S]/(E)
\]
as schemes.  This is a full Keller fiber of rank $N$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We begin on the open set $t\neq0$.  Introduce the coordinates
\[
 S=\frac{x}{t},
 \qquad Q=y+xq,
 \qquad D=1-SQ+\Pi S^2=t^{-1}.
\]
A direct differentiation gives
\begin{equation}
 \det\frac{\partial(\Pi,S,Q)}{\partial(x,y,z)}=D^{-1}.
 \label{eq:source-chart-jacobian}
\end{equation}
Next define
\begin{equation}
 \beta(\Pi,S)
 =\frac{G_\Pi'(S)/g_1-1-\Pi S^2}{S}.
 \label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}
The numerator is divisible by $S$.  Substituting the definitions into
\eqref{eq:gauge-map} gives the two identities
\begin{equation}
 B=Q+\beta(\Pi,S),
 \qquad
 C=\frac{2G_\Pi(S)}{g_1}-BS^2.
 \label{eq:marked-line-identities}
\end{equation}
The second identity is precisely $E_{\Pi,B,C}(S)=0$.  Differentiating it gives
\begin{equation}
 D=\frac{G_\Pi'(S)}{g_1}-BS
  =\frac{E_{\Pi,B,C}'(S)}{g_1}.
 \label{eq:D-derivative}
\end{equation}
At fixed $\Pi$, the Jacobian of \eqref{eq:marked-line-identities} with respect
to $(S,Q)$ is
\begin{equation}
 \det\frac{\partial(B,C)}{\partial(S,Q)}=-2D.
 \label{eq:plane-jacobian}
\end{equation}
The factors $D^{-1}$ and $-2D$ cancel.  Hence $\det DF_G=-2$ on the dense open
set $t\neq0$, and therefore everywhere.

We now identify the entire fiber, not just its field-valued points.  Fix
$(\pi,b,c)$ and abbreviate $E=E_{\pi,b,c}$.  Let $A$ be an arbitrary
commutative $K$-algebra.  An $A$-point $(x,y,z)$ of the fiber satisfies
\[
 tq=\pi.
\]
Because $\pi$ is a unit, both $t$ and $q$ are units.  The chart coordinates
\[
 S=xt^{-1},
 \qquad Q=y+xq,
 \qquad D=t^{-1}
\]
are therefore defined on every point of the fiber.  Equations
\eqref{eq:marked-line-identities} and \eqref{eq:D-derivative} give
\[
 Q+\beta(\pi,S)=b,
 \qquad E(S)=0,
 \qquad D=\frac{E'(S)}{g_1}.
\]
Thus every source point gives a root of $E$ in $A$.

Conversely, suppose $s\in A$ satisfies $E(s)=0$.  Squarefreeness and
characteristic zero make $E$ separable, so Lemma~\ref{lem:derivative-unit}
shows that $E'(s)$ is a unit.  Set
\[
 d=\frac{E'(s)}{g_1},
\]
and define
\[
 Q=b-\beta(\pi,s),
 \qquad
 t=d^{-1},
 \qquad
 x=sd^{-1},
 \qquad
 y=Q-\pi s,
\]
followed by
\[
 q=\pi d,
 \qquad
 z=d^2\left(q-\frac{g_1}{g_3}y^2(1+3t)\right).
\]
The derivative identity says exactly that
\[
 d=1-sQ+\pi s^2.
\]
A calculation in the ring $A$ now gives
\[
 1+xy=t,
 \qquad
 t^2z+\frac{g_1}{g_3}y^2(1+3t)=q,
 \qquad tq=\pi.
\]
The first identity in \eqref{eq:marked-line-identities} gives $B=b$, and the
root equation gives $C=c$.  We have reconstructed a point of the original
fiber.

The two procedures undo one another.  Starting with a source point, one
recovers
\[
 D=t^{-1},\qquad x=S/D,\qquad y=Q-\pi S,\qquad q=\pi D,
\]
and then $z$ from the definition of $q$.  Starting with a root, the marked
coordinate is again $s$.

All formulas use ring operations and the inverse of the explicitly proved unit
$d$.  They therefore commute with homomorphisms $A\to A'$ of commutative
$K$-algebras.  The universal property of $K[S]/(E)$ identifies its algebra maps
to $A$ with roots of $E$ in $A$, giving the natural equivalence
\eqref{eq:natural-fiber-equivalence}.  Yoneda's lemma turns this into the
claimed scheme isomorphism.  In particular, no localization or nilpotent
structure has been discarded.

It remains to check the generic degree.  Put
\[
 L=K(\Pi,B),\qquad \lambda=\frac{g_1}{2},
\]
and write
\[
 E_{\Pi,B,C}(S)=H_{\Pi,B}(S)-\lambda C\in L[C][S].
\]
This polynomial has degree $N$ in $S$.  Its leading coefficient is $g_3\Pi$
when $N=3$ and $g_N\Pi^N$ when $N\geq4$, so it is a unit of $L[C]$.  Hence
$E_{\Pi,B,C}$ is primitive over the UFD $L[C]$.

Assume it factors nontrivially over $L(C)[S]$.  Gauss's lemma then gives a
factorization in $L[C][S]$.  Since the polynomial is linear in $C$, one factor,
say $A(S)$, is independent of $C$.  Comparing the coefficient of $C$ shows
that $A(S)$ divides the nonzero scalar $-\lambda$ in $L[S]$.  Thus $A$ is a
unit, a contradiction.  Therefore $E_{\Pi,B,C}$ is irreducible of degree $N$
over $K(\Pi,B,C)$.  The generic reconstruction identifies the source function
field with the corresponding degree-$N$ extension, and hence
$\gdeg(F_G)=N$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Which hypotheses are used where]\label{rem:gauge-hypotheses}
The fiber reconstruction uses less than the construction of the displayed
map.  It needs $\pi$ to be a unit, $E$ to be separable, and the identity
\[
 E'(S)=g_1\bigl(1-bS+S\beta(\pi,S)+\pi S^2\bigr).
\]
Once the coefficient $g_1/g_3$ in the source formula is available, no further
polynomial identity is needed for the root-to-source reconstruction.  The
condition $g_3\neq0$ is what makes that source coefficient meaningful.  For a
translated polynomial $P(a+S)-P(a)$, the cubic coefficient is the third Hasse
derivative at $a$, equal to $P'''(a)/3!$ in characteristic zero.  The Lean
development follows the same separation.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[Jacobian-one effective form]\label{cor:effective-gauge}
Let
\[
 L(\Pi,B,C)=\left(\Pi,-\frac{B}{2},C\right),
 \qquad \widetilde F_G=L\circ F_G.
\]
Then
\[
 \det D\widetilde F_G=1,
 \qquad \gdeg(\widetilde F_G)=N.
\]
For every $(\pi,b,c)\in K^*\times K^2$ for which $E_{\pi,b,c}$ is
squarefree of degree $N$,
\[
 \widetilde F_G^{-1}\left(\pi,-\frac b2,c\right)
 \simeq\Spec K[S]/(E_{\pi,b,c}).
\]
In particular, a target with $b=0$ is unchanged by the normalization.
Moreover,
\[
 \deg\Pi\leq7,
 \qquad \deg B\leq6N+2,
 \qquad \deg C\leq6N,
\]
so
\[
 \boxed{\max_i\deg(\widetilde F_G)_i\leq6N+2.}
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The linear map $L$ has determinant $-1/2$, so the determinant, geometric
degree, and fiber statements follow from Proposition~\ref{prop:gauge}.

For the degree estimate, $\deg t=2$ and $\deg q\leq5$, hence
$\deg\Pi\leq7$.  A summand of $B$ indexed by $k\geq4$ has degree at most
\[
 2\deg t+(k-2)+k\deg q\leq4+(k-2)+5k=6k+2.
\]
The preceding terms have degrees at most $1$, $6$, and $7$, so
$\deg B\leq6N+2$.  Similarly, $(xq)^k$ has degree at most $6k$, while the
remaining terms of $C$ have degree at most $4$.  Thus $\deg C\leq6N$.
A linear output normalization does not change these bounds.
\end{proof}
