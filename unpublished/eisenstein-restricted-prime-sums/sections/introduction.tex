\section{Starting with Euler's problem}
Our starting point is the theorem of Howe, Huffman, Kohlmeyer and Kruer
\cite[Theorem~1.1]{Euler}: every positive integer congruent to $2$ modulo
$4$ is a sum of two positive integers whose prime factors are all $1$
modulo $4$, with $1$ allowed. These are the split primes for the Gaussian
integers. Their proof uses the square-lattice norm $x^2+y^2$ to find
many candidate pairs, then shows that the unwanted primes cannot spoil
all of them.

This suggests a natural variation: replace the square lattice by the
triangular lattice of the Eisenstein integers. Its norm is
\[
Q(x,y)=x^2-xy+y^2,
\]
and the split primes are now $1$ modulo $3$. Let
\[
\AA=\{a\in\Z_{>0}:p\mid a\Longrightarrow p\equiv1\pmod3\}.
\]
We include $1$, which has no prime factors. The first few values are
$1,7,13,19,31,37,43,49$. Every member of $\AA$ is $1$ modulo $6$,
so two of them can only add to an integer $2$ modulo $6$. The first
extension of the Gaussian result is that this is the only restriction.
For example, $8=1+7$ and $20=7+13$.

The norm form already points toward another extension. We have
$Q(1,-1)=3$, with coprime coordinates, although $3\notin\AA$. Allowing
this ramified prime once gives the family
\[
3\!\cdot\!\AA=\{3a:a\in\AA\}.
\]
Its members are $3$ modulo $6$, so the mixed sums $a+3b$ belong to the
class $4$ modulo $6$. That congruence also turns out to be sufficient.
The two binary answers can be recorded together.
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
One has
\[
\AA+\AA=\{n\in\Z_{>0}:n\equiv2\pmod6\},\qquad
\AA+(3\!\cdot\!\AA)=\{n\in\Z_{>0}:n\equiv4\pmod6\}.
\]
\end{theorem}
For the mixed identity, $16=13+3\cdot1$ is a small example. The factor
$3$ is kept separate throughout: $3\!\cdot\!\AA$ is a dilation, whereas
$3\AA$ will mean a sum of three members of $\AA$.

\paragraph{Following the two extensions.}
The main proof keeps the counting idea of the Gaussian paper. An
Eisenstein norm is a useful candidate summand, but it may contain a prime
$2$ modulo $3$ to even exponent. We must get rid of those primes
altogether. First we build a family of decompositions that can be counted
exactly. Then we estimate the fraction spoiled by each unwanted prime.
If the sum of those fractions is below one, some decomposition survives.

The mixed case explains why it pays to remove even norm components
exactly before making estimates. It also leaves one extra condition:
the component divisible by $3$ must not be divisible by $9$. A six-point
quotient handles that condition. The same calculation completes the
Eisenstein analogue and the mixed identity, with a finite computation
covering the targets below the explicit threshold $10^8$.

Once both identities are available, the remaining results unfold quite
quickly. The two families $\AA$ and $3\!\cdot\!\AA$ together are exactly
the positive Eisenstein norm values that have a representation with
coprime coordinates. This gives their full binary sumset, and adjoining
copies of $1$ gives the higher sumsets. Every positive integer needs at
most four such primitive norm values, or at most seven summands from
$\AA$; the examples $12$ and $25$ show why those bounds cannot be lowered.
We develop these consequences in Section~\ref{sec:primitive-sumsets},
after proving the binary identities that make them possible.

The norm-counting and torus-normalizer averaging method is adapted from
\cite{Euler}. Ramar\'e--Viswanadham also include primitive L\"oschian
integers in their sieve framework \cite[Introduction]{RV}; their additive
Theorem~1.6 concerns three summands in the primitive Gaussian setting.
The classical spectral input used here is made explicit in
Section~\ref{sec:spectral}. After working through this example, we extract
the reusable counting criterion in Section~\ref{sec:orbit-criterion} and
finish with the balanced-summand question that the present argument leaves
open.

\paragraph{A reduction that helps both problems.}
Put $\PP=\{p\text{ prime}:p\equiv2\pmod3\}$. For either admissible residue
class, write $n=gn_0$, where $g\in\AA$ contains every prime-power factor
of $n$ with prime $1$ modulo $3$. Since $g\equiv1\pmod6$, the target
$n_0$ has the same residue as $n$. A decomposition of either type for
$n_0$ scales by $g$ to the corresponding decomposition of $n$.
We can therefore concentrate on
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reduced}
n\equiv2\text{ or }4\pmod6,\qquad
p\mid n\Longrightarrow p\in\PP.
\end{equation}
This reduction will matter in the divisor estimates and in the exact
calculation at $3$. The finite certificates cover all targets in their
respective ranges, so they need no reduction.
