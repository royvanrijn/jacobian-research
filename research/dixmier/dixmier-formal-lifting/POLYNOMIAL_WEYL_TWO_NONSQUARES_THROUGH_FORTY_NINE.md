# A field constraint at gcd sixteen and the two-nonsquare bound

**Theorem PolynomialWeylShapedGcdSixteenSquareClass.** Let a
nongenerating polynomial Weyl pair over a characteristic-zero field
\(k\), with nonzero scalar commutator, have a subrectangular
presentation over \(k\) of Bernstein-degree gcd \(16\). Suppose
its maximal \(X\)-exponent coefficients in that presentation
are single monomials, with negative \((1,-1)\) endpoint
weights. Then its unordered Bernstein degrees have the form
\(\{16a,16b\}\), with \(a=3j+4,\ b=2j+3,\ j\ge0\), and
\(6(b+1)\) is a square in \(k\). In particular, the shaped
pair with degrees \(48,64\) requires \(6\) to be a square
in \(k\).

**Corollary PolynomialWeylTwoNonsquaresThroughFortyNine.**
If both \(-15\) and \(6\) are nonsquares in \(k\), a polynomial
Weyl pair with nonzero scalar commutator generates when one
member has Bernstein degree at most \(49\), or has nonzero
constant differential leading coefficient and order at
most \(49\).

The first assertion concerns a subrectangular presentation
over the ground field. An existence theorem for a standard
presentation after algebraic extension alone does not provide
this field hypothesis.

## Descent of the scalar face to the ground field

Normalize the commutator to one. The [gcd-sixteen residual](POLYNOMIAL_WEYL_BERNSTEIN_DEGREE_THROUGH_THIRTY_THREE.md)
applies after extension of constants. The given presentation
already satisfies the standard minimal hypotheses, since
the pair attains the lower bound sixteen and the signs
are part of the assumption. Its crossing has corner
\((4,12)\) and direction \((4,-1)\).
The common face factor is \(X L(XY^4)\), with
\(L(z)\) proportional to \((z-u)^3\), \(u\ne0\).
The factor is defined over \(k\) up to scale by the
coprime comparison of the two top powers: normalize
their same highest monomial and take a ratio of integer
powers using a Bézout relation for \(a,b\). Hence
\(L\) can be taken over \(k\), and its unique root
\(u\) belongs to \(k\).

Put \(t=X^{1/4}\) and \(U=tY\), so
\([U,t]=(1/4)t^{-2}\). Let \(\lambda^4=u\) be
the root used in the first shift, with a sign chosen
to center that root. The shift is \(U\mapsto U+\lambda\).
In the coefficient extension \(k(\lambda)\) consider
\[
                        \widetilde t=t/\lambda,\qquad
                        \widetilde U=U/\lambda.
\]
Their relation is
\([\widetilde U,\widetilde t]=\widetilde t^{-2}/(4u)\).
The original \(X=u\widetilde t^4\) and
\(Y=\widetilde t^{-1}\widetilde U\) belong to the
\(k\)-algebra generated by \(\widetilde t^{\pm1},\widetilde U\).
The first shift preserves that \(k\)-algebra since it
adds one to \(\widetilde U\). Hence in the \(t,U\) normal
form each coefficient of \(t^r U^q\) belongs to
\(\lambda^{-(r+q)}k\).

The gcd-sixteen proof allows one intervening commuting
single-root shift at slope \(1/2\) in the same
fractional lattice. At that slope the variable on
the face is \(tU\), so adjacent monomials have \(t\)
and \(U\) exponents differing by one each. The unique
root \(\nu\) of the face polynomial in \(tU\) is
minus the ratio of its two top coefficients divided
by its degree. The coefficient classes just proved
give \(\nu\in\lambda^2 k\).
Thus \(U\mapsto U+\nu/t\) still preserves the same
\(k\)-algebra in the rescaled variables. The coefficient
classes persist also in this case.

Order the pair so that \(a=3j+4\) and \(b=2j+3\),
and put \(s=(7j+9)/(4a)\), the necessary scalar
successor slope. After relabeling the elements if
needed, the bracket changes at most by a sign.
In the variable \(\eta=YX^s\) and \(z=\eta^a\),
its leading symbols have the form
\[
                        X^{aw}F(z),\qquad X^{bw}\eta G(z),
\]
where \(F,G\) have degrees three and two, and
\(w=1/(4a)\). Write \(K=4j+5\). Then
\(\eta=Ut^{K/a}\), so the first symbol has terms
\(t^{1+Kq} U^{aq}\), the second
\(t^{2+Kq}U^{1+aq}\). The coefficient classes give
\[
      F(z)=\lambda^{-1}F_k(\lambda^{-(a+K)}z),\qquad
      G(z)=\lambda^{-3}G_k(\lambda^{-(a+K)}z)
\]
with \(F_k,G_k\in k[z]\). This is one common
argument rescaling, so the scalar bracket equation
holds up to nonzero constants for a pair of
polynomials over \(k\):
\[
              b z F'_kG_k-F_k(G_k+a zG'_k)=c\ne0 .
                                                            \tag{1}
\]
Their constant terms are nonzero by (1) at zero.
Normalize both to one, so \(c=-1\).
If the linear coefficient of \(G_k\) were zero,
the linear coefficient of \(F_k\) would also be
zero, and the cubic coefficient equation would
make \(\deg F_k<3\). Therefore rescale \(z\) over
\(k\) to arrange
\[
       G_k=1+z+v z^2,\qquad F_k=1+A z+B z^2+C z^3 .
\]
From (1) and \(2a+1=3b\), its coefficients give
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 A&=\frac{3b+1}{2(b-1)},\\
 (2b-1) B&=3bv+\frac{b+1}{2}A,\\
 (3b-1) C&=2bAv-\frac{b-1}{2}B,\qquad aC=bBv .
 \end{aligned}
\]
Eliminating \(B,C\) yields
\[
                   48b^2v^2-24b(b+1)v+(b+1)(3b+1)=0 .
\]
Its discriminant is \(384b^2(b+1)=(8b)^2\,6(b+1)\).
Since \(v\in k\), the discriminant is a square in \(k\).
For \(b=3\), this requires \(6\) to be a square in \(k\).

## The two new normalized corners of sum twenty-four

Assume now that neither \(-15\) nor \(6\) is a square.
By the [nonsquare bound through forty-seven](POLYNOMIAL_WEYL_BERNSTEIN_FORTY_TWO_REAL_BOUND.md),
both Bernstein degree and constant-leading order at
most \(47\) force generation over \(k\). The general
infinity-shear and dividing-mate argument therefore
gives \(\operatorname{ord}P+\deg\operatorname{lc}P\ge48\)
for a nongenerating member with nonconstant leading
coefficient.

Suppose such a pair has a member of Bernstein degree
\(48\). Apply the quadratic bracket-mate argument
over \(k\) as in the degree-forty-two proof, then the
axis orientation and leading-degree saturation
argument. A pure leading monomial is excluded by
the two lower bounds. In the mixed case orient the
smaller exponent in the derivative. Its actual order
plus leading degree is \(48\). Reduce the partner in
differential order with this member fixed. A smaller
dividing order would give the impossible bound \(96\)
by the leading-coefficient relation. The nondividing
orders \(ga,gb\) and common leading factor of degree
\(d\) now satisfy \(48=a(g+d)\), \(g+d\ge16\).
Thus \(a=2\) or \(3\). The infinity shear over \(k\)
gives the actual subrectangular presentation used above.
If \(a=3\), the gcd is \(16\), so necessarily \(b=4\)
and the square-class theorem contradicts the field
hypothesis.

It remains to take \(a=2,\ g+d=24\).
The arbitrary-pair crossing argument of the
[degree-forty-one proof](POLYNOMIAL_WEYL_BERNSTEIN_DEGREE_THROUGH_FORTY_ONE.md#the-normalized-crossings-up-to-sum-twenty)
applies to this shaped pair. The previous exclusions
for sums at most \(23\) depend only on the relevant
crossing profile and the ratio \(2:b\). At sum \(21\)
the nonsquare \(-15\) obstruction applies over \(k\),
as the current subrectangular presentation is defined
there. Hence only a corner of normalized sum \(24\)
remains.

The integer tests of Guccione–Guccione–Valqui,
Proposition 7.3, at this sum give exactly
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
 C_0 & C_1 & (f_1,f_2) &(\rho,\sigma)\\ \hline
 (8,16)&(3,1)&(2,4)&(3,-1)\\
 (9,15)&(2,1)&(3,5)&(2,-1).
\end{array}
\]
For an initial corner with \(C_1=(h,j)\) and
common factor \(R=X^hY^jL(XY^\rho)\), use the
homogeneous bracket-mate equation (1) in the
[degree-forty-one face calculation](POLYNOMIAL_WEYL_BERNSTEIN_DEGREE_THROUGH_FORTY_ONE.md#the-new-faces-and-their-successors).
Its \(G\) degree is
\(((\rho-1)\deg L-h+j)/(\rho h-j)\), and \(G\) has
a simple zero at every distinct root of \(L\).
A root multiplicity \(m\) is impossible if
\(\rho h-j=(\rho-1)m\). For \((8,16)\)
one has \(\deg L=5,\ \deg G=1\), so its
unique nonzero root is the maximal root of
multiplicity five. For \((9,15)\) one has
\(\deg L=7,\ \deg G=2\), and root multiplicity
three is excluded. It follows that the largest
root multiplicity \(\mu\) is \(5,6\), or \(7\).

Apply [Guccione–Guccione–Valqui II, Proposition 4.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1205.6827v1)
to these edges. Its hypotheses hold because the
two nonmonomial faces have positive nondividing
ratio, zero bracket, scalar commutator and negative
diagonal differences at their highest \(Y\)-endpoints.
Use the successor conventions of the
[degree-forty-one proof](POLYNOMIAL_WEYL_BERNSTEIN_DEGREE_THROUGH_FORTY_ONE.md#the-new-faces-and-their-successors):
\(l\) is the fractional lattice denominator,
\(C=(C_x,\mu)\) the normalized endpoint of largest
\(Y\)-exponent, and \(\beta=l(\mu-C_x)\).
The first successor data are
\[
 \begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c}
 C_0&\mu&C&l&\beta\\ \hline
 (8,16)&5&(13/3,5)&3&2\\
 (9,15)&5,6,7&((\mu+3)/2,\mu)&2&\mu-3.
 \end{array}
\]
Since \(\beta\le\mu\), scalar successors are impossible.
A single-root successor requires integral \(ls\),
where \(s\) is the new slope. Such a shift preserves
\(C,l\) and strictly increases the slope. No such
slope is possible in the second row; in the first
there can only be one at \(2/3\).

For a multiple-root successor apply the finite
lattice test of the earlier proof, including the
projection condition \(M\equiv1\bmod D\) from the
[degree-forty-two residual](POLYNOMIAL_WEYL_BERNSTEIN_FORTY_TWO_REAL_BOUND.md#testing-the-commuting-successors).
With \(j_0\) the least exponent of the face polynomial
in \(\eta\), the rows are
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c}
 l&\mu&M&D&j_0\\ \hline
 3&5&5&2&1,3\\
 2&5&5&2&1,3\\
 2&6&4&3&0,3\\
 2&6&6&5&1\\
 2&7&7&3&1,4\\
 2&7&21&5&2 .
\end{array}
\]
In every row with \(M=\mu\) and a simple root
the multiplier one rules it out. For \(\mu=5\)
the least exponent one is itself simple, and
least exponent three leaves a binomial of degree
two with simple roots. For \(\mu=6,\ M=6\) all
roots are simple. For \(\mu=7,\ M=7\) the least
exponent one is simple, and least exponent four
leaves a binomial of degree three with simple roots.
The row \(\mu=7,\ M=21\) gives
\(f=\eta^2(\eta^5-u_0)\). Its multiplier is three,
and \(1/f^3\) has a nonzero residue at zero, from
the coefficient of \(\eta^5\) in
\((\eta^5-u_0)^{-3}\).

It remains to examine \(\mu=6,\ M=4,\ D=3\)
at slope \(2/3\). With \(j_0=0\), both roots in the
quadratic polynomial in \(\eta^3\) must coincide,
since otherwise there would be six distinct roots
and \(M=4\). Then \(f=(\eta^3-u_0)^2\),
and a maximal-root shift gives \(C'=(11/6,2)\),
\(l=6,\ \beta=1\). Scalar successors are impossible.
A pure single-root edge can only occur at \(5/6\).
The multiple-root test gives the sole row \(M=2,D=1\),
at \(5/6\), with multiplier one and simple roots.
A single-root shift at that slope keeps the corner
and denominator, and leaves no larger successor.

If instead \(j_0=3\), then \(f=\eta^3(\eta^3-u_0)\)
and the maximal root is zero. The identity shift
keeps the actual lattice denominator \(l=2\).
Its new endpoint is \(C'=(5/2,3),\ \beta=1\),
and the slope must exceed \(2/3\). The scalar
and single-root possibilities are excluded.
The only remaining multiple-root row is
\(M=3,D=2,j_0=1\), again with simple roots and
multiplier one. Thus sum twenty-four is impossible.
Every successor corner here has negative diagonal
difference; Proposition 4.3 continues to apply
with the preserved edge, bracket and ratio
hypotheses at every commuting step.

Bernstein degree \(48\) is therefore excluded
over the two-nonsquare field. Bernstein mate
descent excludes degree \(49\), since a
nondividing proper pair with smaller degree
\(49\) cannot have degree gcd at least \(16\).
The constant-leading proof for orders \(48,49\)
now uses the same weighted three-way reduction as
at orders \(42\) through \(47\). The binomial
and intermediate coordinate changes give
orders at most \(24\); a nonconstant leading
coefficient in the intermediate change makes
order plus leading degree at most the starting
order, contrary to the new Bernstein bound
after infinity shear. This gives both conclusions
through \(49\).

## Exact finite checks

The following reproduces the sum-twenty-four corner table
and the successor lattice calculations.

~~~python
from fractions import Fraction
from math import gcd

rows = []
for u in range(3, 12):
    v = 24 - u
    divisor = gcd(u, v)
    for k in range(1, divisor):
        f1, f2 = k*u//divisor, k*v//divisor
        if f1 < 2:
            continue
        d = gcd(f1-1, f2-1)
        rho, sigma = (f2-1)//d, (1-f1)//d
        for r0 in range(1, u):
            for s0 in range(r0):
                if rho*(r0-u)+sigma*(s0-v):
                    continue
                delta = Fraction(v-s0, rho)-s0
                x2, y2 = r0-delta*Fraction(sigma,rho), s0+delta
                if d == 1 and y2 >= 2 and x2 == y2-Fraction(1,rho):
                    continue
                rows.append(((u,v),(r0,s0),(f1,f2),(rho,sigma)))
assert rows == [
    ((8,16),(3,1),(2,4),(3,-1)),
    ((9,15),(2,1),(3,5),(2,-1))
]

def lattice(ell, mu, beta, prev):
    out = []
    for M in range(2,beta*mu+2):
        slope = 1-Fraction(beta*M,ell*mu*(M-1))
        if not prev < slope < 1-Fraction(beta,ell*mu):
            continue
        D = (ell*slope).denominator
        if (M-1) % D:
            continue
        for j in range(mu):
            if (mu-j) % D == 0:
                out.append((M,D,j))
    return out

assert lattice(3,5,2,Fraction(1,3)) == [(5,2,1),(5,2,3)]
assert lattice(2,5,2,Fraction(1,2)) == [(5,2,1),(5,2,3)]
assert lattice(2,6,3,Fraction(1,2)) == [(4,3,0),(4,3,3),(6,5,1)]
assert lattice(2,7,4,Fraction(1,2)) == [(7,3,1),(7,3,4),(21,5,2)]
assert lattice(6,2,1,Fraction(2,3)) == [(2,1,0),(2,1,1)]
assert lattice(2,3,1,Fraction(2,3)) == [(3,2,1)]
~~~
