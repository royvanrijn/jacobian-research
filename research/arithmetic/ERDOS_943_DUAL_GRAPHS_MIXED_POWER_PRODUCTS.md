# Dual graphs for unrestricted mixed-power products

Fix integers \(k,j\ge2\), and let
\[
 {\cal P}_{k,j}=\{a^k b^j:a,b\in\mathbb N\}.
\]
Equivalently, every prime valuation belongs to the additive semigroup generated by \(k,j\). The set is symmetric in the two exponents. Let \(r_{k,j}(N)\) count ordered positive value-pairs from this set with sum \(N\).

**Theorem 1.** For every fixed \(k,j\ge2\), every pair of positive integers \(\kappa_1,\kappa_2\), every \(N\ge1\), and every \(\epsilon>0\), both the ordered value count
\[
 \#\{(U,V)\in{\cal P}_{k,j}^2:\kappa_1U+\kappa_2V=N\}
\]
and the number of positive parameter quadruples satisfying
\(\kappa_1a^kb^j+\kappa_2c^kd^j=N\) are
\[
 \ll_{k,j,\epsilon}
 \begin{cases}
 N^{3/(2(k+j))+\epsilon},&\gcd(k,j)=1,\\
 N^\epsilon,&\gcd(k,j)\ge2.
 \end{cases}
\]
No coprimality or powerfree condition is imposed on these original presentations. The constants are independent of the positive coefficients. In particular the coefficient-one case gives the bound for \(r_{k,j}(N)\).

We first prove the uniform primitive estimate for the intermediate equation
\[
 \kappa_1a^kb^j+\kappa_2c^kd^j=n,\qquad
 \gcd(\kappa_1a^kb^j,\kappa_2c^kd^j)=1.                    \tag{1}
\]
The full coefficient-uniform normalization is supplied in Section 8.

When the exponents are coprime, the proof uses canonical presentations with \(b,d\) \(k\)-powerfree. In these coordinates the five-estimate exponent menu has a unique balanced maximizer. This is a statement about that menu, not a lower bound on representation counts or an assertion about noncanonical presentation boxes.

## 1. Canonical kernels and value fibers

**Lemma 2.** If \(\gcd(k,j)=1\), every value in \({\cal P}_{k,j}\) has a unique presentation \(a^kb^j\) with \(b\) \(k\)-powerfree, meaning \(0\le v_p(b)<k\) at every prime. For arbitrary fixed \(k,j\), a positive value \(U\) has at most \(\tau(U)\) unrestricted presentations \(U=a^kb^j\).

For existence, write a prime exponent of an original \(b\) as \(ks+v\), \(0\le v<k\), and move \(js\) into the exponent of \(a\). For uniqueness, the value exponent \(e\) determines \(v\) from
\[
                          jv\equiv e\pmod k,               \tag{2}
\]
then determines the nonnegative exponent \((e-jv)/k\) in \(a\). Existence of a nonnegative answer follows from the constructive reduction. Coprimality of \(k,j\) is essential here.

For the last assertion, every possible \(b\) divides \(U\); each fixes \(a^k=U/b^j\), hence at most one positive \(a\). The bound does not require a powerfree condition.

A divisor of a \(k\)-powerfree integer, and its quotient by a divisor, are \(k\)-powerfree. A removed gcd may share primes with a reduced factor. None of the graph arguments below assumes otherwise.

Until Section 7 suppose \(\gcd(k,j)=1\) and use the canonical presentations in (1). Every factor of either summand is a unit modulo \(n\). Within an actual representation, each left factor is coprime to each right factor. Nonempty equations have \(n\ge2\), \(\kappa_i<n\), and all parameters at most \(n\).

## 2. Coefficient-uniform fixed-pair multiplicities

For every fixed positive \(P,Q\) and fixed degree \(e\ge3\), the primitive equation
\[
                            P X^e+QY^e=n
\]
has \(O_{e,\epsilon}(n^\epsilon)\) positive solutions, uniformly in \(P,Q\). Apply Evertse's 1985 Theorem 6 to the primes dividing \(n\). The form has \(e\) distinct complex projective linear factors: \(P t^e+Q\) has simple nonzero roots and the homogeneous form has no root at infinity. Its degree is fixed and at least three. The theorem supplies a bound \(C_e^{1+\omega(n)}\), independent of coefficient height. No irreducibility hypothesis is substituted for its distinct-factor condition. Primitive actual summands give primitive coordinates.

Here is the required degree-two input locally.

**Lemma 3.** For squarefree \(D\ge1\) and positive integer \(M\),
\[
 \#\{(x,y)\in\mathbb Z^2:x^2+Dy^2=M\}
                         \ll \tau(M)2^{\omega(M)},          \tag{3}
\]
with an absolute constant. Consequently \(P X^2+QY^2=n\) with coprime positive summands has \(O_\epsilon(n^\epsilon)\) positive solutions uniformly in the coefficients.

Remove \(s=\gcd(x,y)>0\), so \(s^2\mid M\). The new pair \((x',y')\) is primitive and has norm \(m=M/s^2\). A prime dividing \(y'\) cannot divide \(m\), so \(y'\) is invertible modulo \(m\). When \(m>1\), the residue \(x'/y'\) solves \(r^2=-D\pmod m\). An odd prime away from \(D\) has at most two roots at every prime power; the dyadic unit case has at most four. If \(p\mid D,m\), primitiveness forces \(p\mid x'\), \(p\nmid y'\), and the norm has valuation exactly one. Thus \(v_p(m)=1\), with just the zero root modulo \(p\), including \(p=2\). CRT bounds all classes by \(2^{\omega(m)+1}\). For \(m=1\), use one class.

Two primitive solutions \((x',y'),(X,Y)\) in one class have an integral quotient
\[
 \frac{x'+y'\sqrt{-D}}{X+Y\sqrt{-D}}
   =\frac{x'X+Dy'Y+(y'X-x'Y)\sqrt{-D}}m
                       \in\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-D}].
\]
Both numerator coefficients are divisible by \(m\), using \(r^2=-D\). Its norm is one. Integral norm-one elements satisfy \(u^2+Dv^2=1\), so there are at most four. This includes zero coordinates and the modulus-one case. Each class therefore has at most four solutions. Sum over the at most \(\tau(M)\) possible \(s\) to prove (3).

For the coefficient equation, nonempty coprime summands imply \(\gcd(P,Q)=1\) and \(P,Q<n\). Write \(P=P_0u^2,Q=Q_0v^2\), with \(P_0,Q_0\) squarefree. Then \(D=P_0Q_0\) is squarefree, and the injective map
\[
                  x=P_0uX,\quad y=vY
\]
has norm \(x^2+Dy^2=P_0n\le n^2\). Apply (3). The usual bounds \(\tau(m)C^{\omega(m)}\ll_{C,\eta}m^\eta\), for fixed \(C,\eta>0\), give the consequence. These divisor estimates follow by separating the finitely many small primes.

Thus fixing \(b,d\) in (1) leaves a subpower number of pairs \(a,c\), by degree \(k\); fixing \(a,c\) leaves a subpower number of \(b,d\), by degree \(j\). Degree two in EITHER role uses Lemma 3; a powerfree restriction only discards solutions. Coefficients in each quadratic equation are coprime because the original summands are coprime.

## 3. Primitive fractions at every height

**Lemma 4.** One residue class modulo \(n\) contains \(O(1+H^2/n)\) positive reduced fractions \(u/v\), with \(u,v\le H\) and \(\gcd(v,n)=1\).

Represent a fraction by its primitive positive vector. Distinct fractions in one class have nonzero cross determinant divisible by \(n\). Vector lengths are at most \(\sqrt2H\), so
\[
                 |\sin(\theta-\theta')|\ge n/(2H^2).
\]
If the right side exceeds one there cannot be two vectors. Otherwise their angular separation is at least \(n/(2H^2)\). Ordering angles in the first quadrant proves the explicit bound \(1+\pi H^2/n\). Collinear primitive positive vectors give the same fraction.

For each fixed \(\ell\ge2\), a unit modulo \(n\) has at most \(C_\ell^{\omega(n)}\ll_{\ell,\epsilon}n^\epsilon\) \(\ell\)-th roots. For odd prime powers use the cyclic unit group; its kernel has size at most \(\ell\). At powers of two its size is at most \(2\ell\). Each nonempty fiber is a coset of the kernel, and CRT completes the count.

## 4. Two graphs with actual selected labels

Restrict (1) to dyadic intervals \([A,2A),[B,2B),[C,2C),[D,2D)\) for \(a,b,c,d\). A nonempty box has
\[
                         A^kB^j,\ C^kD^j\le n.
\]
Write \(R\) for the number of all representations in the box.

### The kernel graph

Its vertices are \(b,d\); select one actual root-label pair on each edge. Fix distinct \(b_1,b_2\) with common neighbor \(d\). Write
\[
 a_i=\lambda r_i,\qquad c_i=\mu s_i,\qquad
 \gcd(r_1,r_2)=\gcd(s_1,s_2)=1.
\]
Within-edge coprimality also gives \(\gcd(r_i,s_i)=1\). Thus
\[
       u/v=(a_1c_2)/(a_2c_1)=(r_1s_2)/(r_2s_1)             \tag{4}
\]
is reduced, has height at most \(4AC\), and has unit denominator modulo \(n\). Eliminating the common neighbor gives
\[
                           b_1^ju^k\equiv b_2^jv^k\pmod n.
\]
Lemma 4 and the unit-root count give \(n^\epsilon(1+(AC)^2/n)\) possible fractions. Each has divisor-many allocations of the four reduced factors. The exact equation is
\[
 \kappa_1\lambda^k(b_1^ju^k-b_2^jv^k)
                               =n(s_2^k-s_1^k).            \tag{5}
\]
The bracket cannot vanish. Its vanishing would force \(k\mid j(v_p(b_1)-v_p(b_2))\) at each prime. Since \(\gcd(k,j)=1\) and the two kernel valuations lie between zero and \(k-1\), they must coincide at every prime. This contradicts \(b_1\ne b_2\).

Equation (5) therefore determines \(\lambda\) at most once. The first edge then fixes
\[
 \mu^kd^j=
 \frac{n-\kappa_1b_1^j\lambda^kr_1^k}{\kappa_2s_1^k}.
\]
Its canonical \(k\)-powerfree-kernel presentation uniquely recovers \(\mu,d\). Reject nonpositive or nonintegral answers and any disagreement with the selected actual labels. The argument on the other side is identical.

### The root graph

Now use vertices \(a,c\), choosing one actual kernel-label pair per edge. Fix distinct \(a_1,a_2\) sharing \(c\), and write
\[
 b_i=\lambda r_i,\qquad d_i=\mu s_i,\qquad
 \gcd(r_1,r_2)=\gcd(s_1,s_2)=1.
\]
All these kernel gcds and reduced factors are \(k\)-powerfree. There is no assumption that \(\lambda\) is coprime to \(r_i\), or that \(\mu\) is coprime to \(s_i\). Within-edge coprimality makes \(u/v=r_1s_2/(r_2s_1)\) reduced, of height at most \(4BD\), and
\[
                           a_1^ku^j\equiv a_2^kv^j\pmod n.
\]
Its exact reconstruction equation is
\[
 \kappa_1\lambda^j(a_1^ku^j-a_2^kv^j)
                               =n(s_2^j-s_1^j).            \tag{6}
\]
If the bracket vanished in an actual lift, positivity and coprimality would give \(s_1=s_2=1\), hence \(d_1=d_2=\mu\). The remaining equality is \(a_1^kr_1^j=a_2^kr_2^j\). The \(r_i\) are \(k\)-powerfree; Lemma 2 forces \(a_1=a_2,r_1=r_2\), contradicting the distinct root vertices.

Thus (6) fixes \(\lambda\) at most once. The first edge fixes \(c^k\mu^j\), whose unique canonical presentation recovers \(c,\mu\). All additional powerfree, gcd, cap and selection conditions only discard candidates.

The two selected graphs can be chosen independently. Divisor allocations are subpower because all actual parameters and fraction numerators are bounded by fixed powers of \(n\). We have proved
\[
 \Lambda_{\rm ker}\ll n^\epsilon(1+(AC)^2/n),\qquad
 \Lambda_{\rm root}\ll n^\epsilon(1+(BD)^2/n).               \tag{7}
\]

A bipartite graph of side sizes \(m_1,m_2\), with both codegrees at most \(\Lambda\), has
\[
 e\le m_1+m_2+\sqrt\Lambda\min(m_1\sqrt{m_2},m_2\sqrt{m_1}).
\]
Indeed pair-counting and Cauchy--Schwarz give \(e^2/m_2-e\le\Lambda m_1^2\), and the symmetric inequality. Restore the subpower edge multiplicities from Section 2 to obtain the following actual box bounds.

**Proposition 5.** For coprime \(k,j\ge2\), in the canonical parameter box,
\[
\begin{split}
 R&\ll n^\epsilon[B+D+(1+AC/\sqrt n)\min(B\sqrt D,D\sqrt B)],\\
 R&\ll n^\epsilon[A+C+(1+BD/\sqrt n)\min(A\sqrt C,C\sqrt A)], \tag{8}\\
 R&\ll n^\epsilon\min(AC,BD).
\end{split}
\]
The constants depend only on the fixed exponents and \(\epsilon\). These estimates count all actual representations in the box, not just their selected labels.

## 5. The mixed curve and the five exponent bounds

Fix \(a,d\). The remaining variables satisfy
\[
                 P b^j+Q c^k=n,\qquad P,Q>0.
\]
This curve is absolutely irreducible of total degree \(m=\max(k,j)\). A root \(\xi\) of \(n-PX^j\) is nonzero and simple. Viewing \(Y^k-(n-PX^j)/Q\) as a polynomial over \(\mathbb C[X]\), Eisenstein at \(X-\xi\) proves irreducibility. This proof requires no ordering of the exponents.

Bombieri--Pila's 1989 Theorem 5 gives a coefficient-uniform \(O_{m,\eta}(H^{1/m+\eta})\) bound in a square of side \(H\), for fixed \(m\). Its explicit large-\(H\) threshold depends only on \(m\); below it the number of lattice points is bounded by a degree-dependent constant. Enclose each remaining rectangle in a square and also fix \(b,c\) for the other orientation. This gives
\[
 R\ll_{k,j,\epsilon}n^\epsilon
 \min\{AD\max(B,C)^{1/m},\ BC\max(A,D)^{1/m}\}.              \tag{9}
\]
There is no coefficient-height factor or fixed-curve hypothesis here.

Take logarithms to base \(n\) of the four dyadic scales:
\[
 \alpha=\log_n A,\ \beta=\log_n B,\ 
 \gamma=\log_n C,\ \delta=\log_n D,\quad
 X=\alpha+\gamma,\ Y=\beta+\delta.
\]
The coefficient-free outer resources are
\[
 k\alpha+j\beta\le1,\quad k\gamma+j\delta\le1,\quad
 \alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\ge0.                            \tag{10}
\]
Positive coefficients can shrink the actual feasible region; enlarging to (10) supplies a uniform bound. The five estimates are \(X,Y\) and
\[
\begin{split}
 K&=\max\{\beta,\delta,\min(\beta+\delta/2,\delta+\beta/2)+(X-1/2)_+\},\\
 D_*&=\max\{\alpha,\gamma,\min(\alpha+\gamma/2,\gamma+\alpha/2)+(Y-1/2)_+\},\\
 M_*&=\min\{\alpha+\delta+\max(\beta,\gamma)/m,\
                  \beta+\gamma+\max(\alpha,\delta)/m\}.    \tag{11}
\end{split}
\]
Let \(F=\min(X,Y,K,D_*,M_*)\). Finite sums and constants in the original bounds are absorbed by resizing \(\epsilon\).

## 6. Exact maximum and unique maximizer

**Theorem 6.** For every DISTINCT pair \(k,j\ge2\), the function \(F\) on the full closed polytope (10) has
\[
 \max F=t=\frac3{2(k+j)},\qquad
 \operatorname*{argmax}F=
 \left\{\left(\frac1{k+j},\frac1{k+j},
                    \frac1{k+j},\frac1{k+j}\right)\right\}. \tag{12}
\]
No symmetry of the coordinates is assumed. This is a statement about the displayed exponent menu, not an arithmetic lower bound or an assertion about boxes of noncanonical presentations. When \(\gcd(k,j)\ge2\), the separate stronger arithmetic bound proved below makes this menu unnecessary; its algebraic statement remains well-defined.

The transformation
\[
 (k,j;\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)
        \longmapsto(j,k;\beta,\alpha,\delta,\gamma)
\]
preserves resources, swaps \(X,Y\), swaps \(K,D_*\), and swaps the two mixed-curve expressions, because their denominator is \(m\). It suffices to prove the optimization for \(k<j\), when \(m=j\). This transformation concerns the already-derived numerical bounds; it makes no exchange of an arithmetic powerfree constraint. We characterize the entire level set \(F\ge t\), without assuming an upper bound in advance.

Call the root pair mixed if
\[
 \alpha+\gamma/2\ge t,\qquad\gamma+\alpha/2\ge t,
\]
and the kernel pair mixed if
\[
 \beta+\delta/2\ge t,\qquad\delta+\beta/2\ge t.
\]
Write \(R_1=k\alpha+j\beta,R_2=k\gamma+j\delta\).

### Strict individual-coordinate alternatives

These exclusions use only resources, the indicated mixed/individual inequalities and \(M_*\); no zero-height assumption is needed.

Suppose a kernel coordinate is at least \(t\) and the root pair is mixed. Swap summands to take \(\beta\ge t\). Then
\[
 3\ge2R_1+R_2
  =k(2\alpha+\gamma)+j(2\beta+\delta)\ge2(k+j)t=3.
\]
Every inequality is equality. Thus
\[
 \beta=t,\quad\delta=0,\quad\gamma=1/k,\quad
                          \alpha=t-1/(2k).
\]
A negative last coordinate is infeasible. Otherwise \(1/k>t\), and
\[
 M_*\le\alpha+\delta+\max(\beta,\gamma)/j
                        =t-\frac{j-2}{2kj}<t.              \tag{13}
\]

Suppose a root coordinate is at least \(t\) and the kernel pair is mixed. Orient it as \(\alpha\ge t\). The same resource combination forces
\[
 \alpha=t,\quad\gamma=0,\quad\delta=1/j,\quad
                            \beta=t-1/(2j).
\]
Using the OTHER mixed-curve expression gives
\[
 M_*\le t-\frac1{2j}+\frac{\max(t,1/j)}j<t,
\]
since \(t\le3/10<1/2\) and \(1/j\le1/3<1/2\). This also handles \(j>2k\); it does not assume that the opposite coordinate \(1/j\) is at least \(t\).

If both pairs have individual coordinates at least \(t\) on the same summand, that summand uses resource at least \((k+j)t=3/2>1\).

For opposite individual coordinates, first take \(\beta,\gamma\ge t\). Resource substitution into the first mixed expression gives
\[
 M_*\le\frac1k+\frac1j-\frac jk\beta-\frac kj\gamma
                                     +\frac{\max(\beta,\gamma)}j
 \le\frac1k+\frac1j-\left(\frac jk+\frac{k-1}j\right)t.
\]
In either ordering of \(\beta,\gamma\) the two remaining coefficients are negative and their sum is the displayed coefficient sum. If instead \(\alpha,\delta\ge t\), use the second mixed expression and the two orders of \(\alpha,\delta\); exactly the same bound follows. Put
\(\Phi=k^2-kj+j^2-3k\). That upper bound equals
\[
                         t-\frac{\Phi}{2kj(k+j)}<t.        \tag{14}
\]
Indeed \(\Phi\) increases for integer \(j\ge k+1\) and equals \((k-1)^2>0\) at \(j=k+1\). This exhausts both opposite orientations without interchanging the exponents.

### The height losses vanish on the whole level set

If \((k,j)\ne(2,3)\), then \(j\ge4\) and \(Y\le2/j\le1/2\). If \(X>1/2\), the combined resource gives
\[
                Y\le(2-kX)/j<(4-k)/(2j)\le t,
\]
which contradicts \(F\ge t\). The last inequality holds for \(k=2,j\ge4\) and for all \(k\ge3,j>k\); a nonpositive right side already contradicts feasibility. The boundary \(k=2,j=4,X=1/2\) is retained, not silently discarded.

For \((k,j)=(2,3)\), \(t=3/10\). If \(Y\ge1/2\), then \(X\le1/4<t\). Thus \(Y<1/2\) and the root graph has no height loss. Suppose \(X>1/2\). If \(\beta\) or \(\delta\) is at least \(t\), the root graph reaching \(t\) supplies either a mixed root pair or an individual root coordinate. The strict alternatives above exclude each possibility, regardless of the kernel height loss.

Otherwise \(\beta,\delta<t\), so the kernel graph can reach \(t\) only through its square-root term. Since its minimum linear term is at most \(3Y/4\),
\[
 t\le\min\{Y,3Y/4+X-1/2\}
   \le\min\{Y,1/2-3Y/4\}\le2/7<3/10.                     \tag{15}
\]
The middle step uses \(2X+3Y\le2\); the two affine functions meet at \(Y=2/7\). Hence \(X\le1/2\) here too. Both height losses vanish, including all equality endpoints.

### Equality forces the four coordinates

With zero losses, each graph reaching \(t\) supplies either its mixed pair or an individual coordinate at least \(t\). Every alternative involving an individual coordinate has been strictly excluded. Both pairs must therefore be mixed. Adding their inequalities gives \(X,Y\ge4t/3\), and
\[
                  2\ge R_1+R_2=kX+jY\ge(4/3)(k+j)t=2.
\]
All terms are equal. Since \(k,j>0\), \(X=Y=4t/3\). The two mixed inequalities within each pair also have equal sum \(2t\), so both are equalities. Subtracting them yields
\(\alpha=\gamma=\beta=\delta=2t/3=1/(k+j)\).

Conversely this point is feasible with each resource equal to one. Its losses are zero, its two graph bounds equal \(t\), its \(X,Y\) equal \(2/(k+j)>t\), and each mixed-curve bound equals \((2+1/j)/(k+j)>t\) in the smaller-first order. Thus \(F=t\) there. This proves the maximum and uniqueness simultaneously, without importing an earlier three-tenths result.

Equal exponents are deliberately excluded from this optimization theorem. For example at \(k=j=3\), both the balanced point and
\[
                  \alpha=\delta=1/4,\qquad\beta=\gamma=1/12
\]
give minimum \(1/4\). The arithmetic subpower result below covers equal exponents without a unique-optimizer assertion.

## 7. Primitive bounds and unrestricted presentation fibers

For \(\gcd(k,j)=1\), Theorem 6 and the five actual estimates give the canonical primitive box count
\[
                  R\ll_{k,j,\epsilon}n^{3/(2(k+j))+\epsilon}.
\]
There are only \(O_{k,j}((1+\log n)^4)\) boxes: all positive variables are at most \(n\). Reallocate the initial \(\epsilon\)'s to sum them. The constants in Sections 2--5 are uniform in \(\kappa_1,\kappa_2\); none depends on coefficient height.

For every fixed ordered pair of underlying values in (1), Lemma 2 gives at most \(\tau(U)\tau(V)\) unrestricted presentations. Since \(U,V<n\) in a nonempty equation, divisor absorption proves the same bound for arbitrary positive parameter quadruples. This step does not assert that their noncanonical box exponents have the same unique maximizer.

For \(g=\gcd(k,j)\ge2\), no canonical uniqueness or graph reconstruction is needed. Set
\[
                   X=a^{k/g}b^{j/g},\qquad Y=c^{k/g}d^{j/g}.
\]
The primitive summands imply \(\gcd(X,Y)=1\), and
\[
                         \kappa_1X^g+\kappa_2Y^g=n.
\]
For \(g=2\), Lemma 3 applies. For \(g\ge3\), use the coefficient-uniform degree-\(g\) Evertse input. Each gives \(n^\epsilon\) pairs uniformly in the positive coefficients. For fixed \(X,Y\), choose \(b\mid X,d\mid Y\). These choices determine \(a^{k/g}\) and \(c^{k/g}\), hence at most one positive \(a,c\). There are at most \(\tau(X)\tau(Y)\) lifts, absorbed in \(n^\epsilon\). This proves the stronger primitive PARAMETER bound for every common-divisor branch, including \(k=j\). Nonempty positive equations have \(\kappa_i<n\); when \(n=1\) there are no solutions.

## 8. Full positive-coefficient normalization

**Lemma 7.** A coefficient-uniform primitive exponent bound for (1), counting either canonical representatives when available or all presentations, transfers to the full positive-coefficient value equation at every positive original target. For fixed \(k\), normalization costs only a subpower factor, uniformly in the original coefficients.

Fix the ORIGINAL positive coefficients \(\kappa_1,\kappa_2\). For values \(U,V\in{\cal P}_{k,j}\) with \(\kappa_1U+\kappa_2V=N\), set
\[
 h=\gcd(\kappa_1U,\kappa_2V)\mid N,\quad n=N/h,\quad
                         P_1=\kappa_1U/h,\ P_2=\kappa_2V/h.
\]
Then \(P_1,P_2\) are positive and coprime. At each prime \(p\mid h\), write the total quotient exponent \(v_p(P_i)=kq_i+s_i\), \(0\le s_i<k\). Put \(p^{s_i}\) into a NEW coefficient \(\widetilde\kappa_i\), put \(p^{q_i}\) into its new root, and omit \(p\) from the new kernel.

At primes \(p\nmid h\), retain the fixed original coefficient prime part and a factorization of the original value. Thus the new coefficient is exactly
\[
 \widetilde\kappa_i=
 \left(\prod_{p\nmid h}p^{v_p(\kappa_i)}\right)
 \left(\prod_{p\mid h}p^{s_i}\right).                       \tag{16}
\]
The new value \(P_i/\widetilde\kappa_i\) belongs to \({\cal P}_{k,j}\): its prime exponent at \(p\mid h\) is a nonnegative multiple of \(k\); elsewhere it is an exponent from the original value. Canonicalize this value if \(\gcd(k,j)=1\); otherwise its unrestricted primitive parameter bound applies. The two new complete summands are coprime.

For a fixed divisor \(h\), the part of (16) away from \(h\) is DETERMINED by the original coefficients and \(h\), even if those coefficients have prime factors not dividing \(N\). It is not another divisor index. At each prime of \(h\), coprimality of \(P_1,P_2\) permits at most \(2k-1\) joint residue pairs: both zero, or one positive residue in exactly one coefficient. Consequently the number of new coefficient pairs over all \(h\mid N\) is at most
\[
                    \tau(N)(2k-1)^{\omega(N)}
                               \ll_{k,\eta}N^\eta.        \tag{17}
\]
The new coefficients in a nonempty positive normalized equation are less than \(n\); \(n=1\) is empty. The uniform primitive estimate therefore applies without any coefficient-height loss.

A normalized tuple and its new coefficients recover the original ordered values at most once:
\[
 U=\frac{h\widetilde\kappa_1(a')^k(b')^j}{\kappa_1},
 \qquad
 V=\frac{h\widetilde\kappa_2(c')^k(d')^j}{\kappa_2}.
\]
Reject nonintegral answers, nonmembers of the original class, or failure of the prescribed gcd. These tests only discard candidates. Using \(n\le N\) and absorbing (17) with smaller intermediate epsilons proves the FULL original positive-coefficient value bound in Theorem 1.

For each fixed original value-pair, Lemma 2 gives at most \(\tau(U)\tau(V)\) original presentations. Since the original positive coefficients are at least one, \(U,V\le N\); their divisor factors are subpower uniformly. This proves the full unrestricted PARAMETER bound too. No original or new coprimality condition has been retained in Theorem 1. The common-exponent primitive bound from Section 7 gives the stronger full \(N^\epsilon\) conclusion by the same normalization. \(N=1\) gives no positive sum.

For the original coefficient-one problem, (16) reduces to the familiar condition \(\widetilde\kappa_i\mid\operatorname{rad}(h)^{k-1}\). Even then it is wrong in general to require \(\widetilde\kappa_i\mid h\), or to assume that \(h\) is \(k\)-full. For example the \((5,2)\)-product values \(32,4\) have \(h=4\), while the quotient \(32/h=8\) requires residue coefficient \(8\nmid4\). The support count, rather than that false divisibility, is what gives (17).

## 9. The complete square-full specialization and scope of the inputs

**Corollary 8.** For every positive integer \(N\), the ordered count of two positive square-full integers with sum \(N\) is \(O_\epsilon(N^{3/10+\epsilon})\). The coprime form
\[
 \kappa_1a^2b^3+\kappa_2c^2d^3=n,\qquad
 \gcd(\kappa_1a^2b^3,\kappa_2c^2d^3)=1,\quad b,d\ {\rm squarefree}
\]
has the same bound, uniformly in all positive coefficients and positive \(n\). The unique maximizer of its five box exponents on the coefficient-free outer polytope is the point with all four exponents \(1/5\).

Indeed \({\cal P}_{2,3}\) is exactly the positive square-full integers: every prime exponent is zero or at least two, and the unique \(2\)-powerfree-kernel presentation is precisely square-times-squarefree-cube. Use the coprime-exponent primitive proof in Sections 2--6, its explicit optimizer at \((2,3)\), and the coefficient-one specialization of the normalization in Section 8. The common-divisor arithmetic branch, unrestricted-presentation fiber bound, reversed-order transformation, and an older three-tenths theorem are not premises of this specialization. Positive \(n=1,N=1\) give no sums. The optimizer concerns the displayed outer menu, not a coefficient-specific smaller feasible region or an arithmetic lower count.

There are two deep counting inputs:
* Evertse, 1985, Theorem 6, printed page 84, with its supplied derivation on page 85, bounds primitive binary-form values with prescribed prime support when there are at least three distinct complex projective factors. It is coefficient-uniform and does not require irreducibility. Its deep \(S\)-unit input remains imported. [Author primary](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/2463/2463D.pdf).
* Bombieri--Pila, *The Number of Integral Points on Arcs and Ovals* (1989), Theorem 5, author PDF page 17, gives the uniform degree-\(m\) bound used in Section 5. For \(H\ge\exp(m^6)\) its explicit expression is \(H^{1/m}\exp(12\sqrt{m\log H\log\log H})\). Its analytic determinant argument remains imported. [Author primary](https://people.maths.ox.ac.uk/pila/Ovals.pdf).

The local positive norm argument, primitive-fraction packing, actual reconstructions, resource optimization and gcd normalization are supplied above. Heath-Brown's primitive cubic theorem, a full six-cycle hypothesis, and a fixed-difference square-full estimate are not inputs.

The valuation-semigroup class and canonical residue representations already appear in Martin--Yip, *Oscillation results for the summatory functions of fake \(\mu\)'s* (2025), Example 2.11, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4153/S0008414X25101314). That example treats the indicator and its Euler product. It is context, not a premise of this pointwise binary count.

The square-full specialization uses the same two-graph architecture as the repository's [dual-graph proof](ERDOS_943_DUAL_KERNEL_GRAPHS.md). Its separate [all-\(k\)-full bound](POWERFUL_SUM_GRAPHS.md) concerns a different class and is neither a premise nor an automatic consequence here. For example \({\cal P}_{3,4}\) excludes a prime to exponent five, although that value is three-full. The theorem concerns two fixed power generators and positive summands; it is not a statement for all \(k\)-full integers or for growing exponents. No priority or optimality claim is made.
