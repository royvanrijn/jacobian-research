# Frame-independent fibre obstructions at cubic Engel index four

Let \(K\) be any field of characteristic zero. A homogeneous cubic map
\(H:B\to B\) defines a symmetric trilinear product
\(\mu\) by \(H(x)=\mu(x,x,x)\). Put \(L_a b=\mu(a,a,b)\);
then \(JH(a)=3L_a\). An ideal means a subspace \(I\) with
\(\mu(I,B,B)\subseteq I\).

## The intrinsic obstruction

**Lemma FibreObstruction.** Suppose a linear decomposition \(B=U\oplus I\)
puts \(H\) in the form
\[
 H(u,z)=(H_0(u),P(u,z))
\]
and \((JH_0)^3=0\). Then \(I\) is an ideal and
\[
 L_a^3b\in I\qquad(a,b\in B).
\]
If \(P\) has degree at most one in \(z\), then
\(\mu(I,I,B)=0\). If it has degree at most two in \(z\), then
\(\mu(I,I,I)=0\).

Indeed, base independence of \(z\), polarized in characteristic zero, says
that \(I\) is an ideal. The product descends to \(B/I\), where the cube of
every \(L_a\) vanishes. The two fibre-degree statements follow by polarizing
the terms containing respectively at least two and three fibre variables.
These statements are invariant under every invertible linear coordinate
change. Equivalently, every eligible \(I\) contains the ideal generated by
the elements \(L_a^3b\). No nonlinear change of coordinates is asserted.

## Two literal cubic maps

Write \(F=\operatorname{id}-H\). The first example, in seven variables
\((a,b,c,d,e,f,g)\), is
\[
 H=(0,\ a^3,\ 3a^2b,\ 3a^2c,\ 3ac^2,\
       3a^2e-6acd,\ 3a^2f+3ad^2).
\]
The second, in nine variables \((a,b,c,d,e,f,g,h,i)\), is
\[
 H=(0,\ a^3,\ 3a^2b,\ 3a^2c,\ 3ac^2,\ c^3,\
       3a^2f-3c^2d,\ 3a^2g+3cd^2,\
       3a^2h+3c^2e-d^3).
\]

**Theorem EngelFourFibreObstructions.** Both maps satisfy
\((JH)^4=0\) identically and have Jacobian nilpotency index exactly four.
The seven-variable map has no linear decomposition as in the lemma with
cube-zero base and fibre degree at most one. The nine-variable map has no
such decomposition with fibre degree at most two. Their polynomial
inverses have degrees thirteen and twenty-one, respectively.

The full polynomial identities \((JH)^4=0\) are checked by exact multiplication
of the displayed polynomial matrices; the attached standard-library checker
reconstructs the matrices directly from the literal cubics. For both maps,
with \(x=e_0\),
\[
 JH(e_0)^3e_0=27e_3,\qquad L_{e_0}^3e_0=e_3.
\]
Hence every eligible ideal contains \(e_3\). In the first example
\(\mu(e_3,e_3,e_0)=e_6\ne0\); in the second
\(\mu(e_3,e_3,e_3)=-e_8\ne0\). The lemma gives the two contradictions.
In particular these are obstructions in every linear frame, rather than
failures of a selected fibre projection.

For the inverse claims assign positive coordinate weights
\[
 (1,3,5,7,11,13,15),\qquad (1,3,5,7,11,15,17,19,21).
\]
Every cubic has output weight equal to the sum of its input weights and uses
only earlier coordinates. Thus \(F\) is a triangular polynomial automorphism,
and its inverse preserves these weights. Along the line \(s e_0\), recursive
substitution gives inverse-coordinate coefficients
\[
 (1,1,3,9,27,-81,0),\qquad
 (1,1,3,9,27,27,-162,243,729),
\]
with the corresponding powers of \(s\) given by the weights.
In the first case degree fifteen could only be \(a^{15}\) in the last
coordinate, and its coefficient vanishes. Ordinary degrees in each output
are odd, because all input weights are odd and the output weight is odd.
The inverse therefore has degree at most thirteen; the coefficient
\(-81a^{13}\) attains it. In the second case the weight bound gives degree at
most twenty-one, attained by \(729a^{21}\).

The seven-variable cancellation also shows why a nonzero individual
ternary-tree value need not be a nonzero coefficient in the inverse sum.
These examples do not decide whether cubic maps of Jacobian nilpotency
index four admit a dimension-independent inverse-degree bound.
