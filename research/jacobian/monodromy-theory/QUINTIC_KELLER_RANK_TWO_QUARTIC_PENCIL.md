# Quartic factors in a rank-two quintic leading span

**Theorem QuinticKellerRankTwoQuarticPencil.** Let \(F=(P,Q,R)\) be a
three-variable Keller map over \(\mathbb C\) with independent quintic
leading components \(P_5,Q_5\) and a quartic component \(R\).
Then \(R_4\) is reducible. If it has an irreducible cubic factor \(T\)
times a line \(z\), the quintic span is \(\langle z^5,z^2T\rangle\)
after taking that line as a source coordinate. Within that form,
the case \(L=0\) in (5) forces invertibility, and the case \(L\ne0\)
requires \(T(x,y,0)\) proportional to \(L^3\).
If \(R_4\) is a reduced
product of two irreducible conics, its factors
span a pencil containing a double line, and their intersection is
a single point of contact order four. In coordinates one may then
write \(R_4\), up to a nonzero scalar, as
\[
                         (x^2+yz)(x^2+yz+z^2).
\]
The theorem applies in characteristic zero with geometric factorization
over an algebraic closure, by the usual coefficient-field embedding.

The argument uses the projective pencil construction in
[Wang and Yan, Lemma 2.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.36495v1),
adapted to the unequal degrees five, five and four. It also uses their
differential criterion and relative constants in Proposition 2.2 and
Lemma 2.3 of the same paper. The two-conic claim invokes the
[quartic tacnode exclusion](KELLER_QUARTIC_COMPONENT_BOUNDARY_OBSTRUCTION.md)
only after the determinant equations constrain the conic pencil.

## Valuations along a projective pencil

Write \(A=P_5,\ B=Q_5,\ C=R_4\). The degree-eleven part of the constant
determinant gives \(dA\wedge dB\wedge dC=0\). Set \(u=A/B,\ v=C^5/B^4\).
The equation implies \(dB\wedge du\wedge dv=0\). Contraction with the
radial Euler derivation gives \(5B\,du\wedge dv=0\), since \(u,v\)
have degree zero. Hence \(u,v\) are algebraically dependent, and \(u\)
is nonconstant by independence of \(A,B\).

Apply Gordan's theorem to the field generated by \(u,v\) in the
function field of \(\mathbb P^2\) (see
[Petravchuk and Iena, Lemma 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0701588)).
It is \(\mathbb C(p/q)\) for coprime independent ternary forms \(p,q\)
of the same degree \(m\ge1\). Expressing \(u\) as a reduced quotient in
this parameter gives independent coprime binary forms \(A_0,B_0\)
of the same degree \(e\ge1\), and
\[
                 A=g A_0(p,q),\qquad B=g B_0(p,q),\qquad \deg g+me=5 .
 \tag{1}
\]
Indeed the substituted binary forms are coprime: a common irreducible
factor would divide both \(p,q\) because the binary forms have no
common projective zero. Thus \(g\) is polynomial. We also have
\(v=c(p/q)\) for a rational function \(c\).

Let \(\nu_f\) denote the multiplicity of an irreducible polynomial
\(f\) in a polynomial. For each pencil member \(M\) in the span of
\(p,q\), there is a scalar \(c_M\in\mathbb Q\) such that
\[
                    5\nu_f(C)-4\nu_f(g)=c_M\nu_f(M)\qquad(f\mid M).
 \tag{2}
\]
To see this, any factor of \(B_0(p,q)\) in that member is a fixed
multiple of the whole member, and the order of \(c(p/q)\) at \(f\)
is its order at the corresponding parameter multiplied by
\(\nu_f(M)\). These observations include the member \(q=0\).
Two different members are coprime. For a factor outside every
member both contributions vanish. Since \(\deg g\le4\), a factor
of \(g\) or \(C\) cannot satisfy \(5\nu_f(C)=4\nu_f(g)\).
Every such factor therefore lies in one pencil member.

## A cubic or quartic factor

Suppose \(C\) has an irreducible factor \(T\) of degree at least three.
It occurs with multiplicity one and its pencil member has degree
\(m\ge\deg T\). If \(m=5\), then \(g\) is constant and \(T\) has
multiplicity one in its pencil member. Equation (2) makes the whole
member divide \(C\), impossible at degree four.

If \(m=4\), then \(e=1\) and \(g=\ell\) is linear up to scalar.
The member containing \(T\) must be \(C\). When \(T\) is quartic
this is immediate. When \(T\) is cubic, the remaining linear factor
of the member must have \(\nu_f(C)=1,\ \nu_f(g)=0\) by (2), since
the proportionality scalar from \(T\) is five. Thus \(\ell\) is
outside that member. Its own member can contain no other factor
by (2) and is a scalar multiple of \(\ell^4\).

If \(m=3\), write \(C=TL\) with \(L\) linear. Then \(e=1\),
\(\deg g=2\), and \(T\) itself is a pencil member.
The member containing \(L\) is a scalar multiple of \(L^3\).
In fact, if \(\nu_L(g)\le1\), the left side of (2) at \(L\) is
positive, while every other factor of that member has nonpositive
left side. If \(\nu_L(g)=2\), then \(g\) is a scalar multiple of
\(L^2\), and every other factor in that member has left side zero.
Now suppose another member \(M\) contains a factor of \(g\).
Outside \(T\) and \(L^3\), the polynomial \(C\) is absent, so
\(\nu_f(g)/\nu_f(M)\) is a fixed positive rational for all \(f\mid M\).
It is less than one since \(\deg g<\deg M\). Its denominator
therefore divides all the multiplicities in a cubic member and must
be three. This makes \(M\) the cube of another line.
The pencil spanned by two independent cubes of lines would make
\(T\) binary and reducible. Hence \(g\) is a scalar multiple of
\(L^2\).

In both possible degrees \(m=3,4\), the quintic span is
\(\langle \ell^5,\ell C\rangle\) for a linear form \(\ell\),
with \(\ell=L\) in the cubic-pencil case. The following determinant
argument rules out that span when \(C\) is not divisible by \(\ell\)
and \(C|_{\ell=1}\) is non-composite. This excludes the quartic-pencil
case here and leaves the cubic-pencil form stated in the theorem.

## The special quintic span

Normalize the top parts by a constant target change and take
\(z=\ell\), so \(P_5=z^5,\ Q_5=zC\). Put \(S=Q-zR\), of degree at most
four, and let \(\{\cdot,\cdot\}\) be the binary bracket in \(x,y\).
The full determinant identity is
\[
       \kappa=P_z\{S,R\}-S_z\{P,R\}+R_z\{P,S\}-R\{P,R\}.
 \tag{3}
\]
This follows by substituting \(dQ=dS+z\,dR+R\,dz\) into
\(dP\wedge dQ\wedge dR\). In particular its degree-ten equation is
\[
                       \{5z^4 S_4-C P_4,C\}=0 .
 \tag{4}
\]
Set \(r=C(x,y,1)\). If \(r\) is non-composite, the rational constants
of its binary Jacobian derivation are \(\mathbb C(r)\) by the
closed-polynomial criterion of Wang and Yan, Proposition 2.2(ii)
and Lemma 2.3. For a polynomial constant this reduces to
\(\mathbb C[r]\) by a univariate Bézout identity after substitution.

First suppose \(C\) is not divisible by \(z\), so \(\deg r=4\).
Equation (4) then puts \(5S_4(x,y,1)-rP_4(x,y,1)\) in
\(\mathbb C[r]\) of \(r\)-degree at most two. Reducing modulo
\(r\) and using the binary degree bound four makes both
\(S_4(x,y,1),P_4(x,y,1)\) affine linear in \(r\).
Subtract the corresponding multiples of \(R\) from \(P,Q\)
to leave their \(P_4,S_4\) terms dependent only on \(z\).
For \(j=3,2,1\) in descending order, suppose all higher
homogeneous terms of \(P,S\) depend only on \(z\).
The part of degree \(j+6\) of (3) is now
\(\{5z^4 S_j-C P_j,C\}\). The term with \(S_z\) is lower by
at least one degree, and the term with \(R_z\) has degree
at most \(2j+1\). Dehomogenizing as before and using
\(\deg P_j(x,y,1),\deg S_j(x,y,1)<4\) makes both constant.
Thus \(P\) is univariate of degree five and has a critical
point, a contradiction.

This proves the needed exclusion for \(C\) irreducible and
for \(C=TL\) whenever \(\ell\) is outside \(C\). Indeed in those
cases \(r\) is non-composite: if a degree-four polynomial is
a proper composition, its irreducible factors have degree at most
two, whereas the dehomogenized \(T\) is still irreducible and
has degree at least three.

## Further restrictions in the cubic-pencil case

Suppose \(C=zT\) with \(T\) irreducible cubic. Now \(r=T(x,y,1)\)
is an irreducible cubic, and (4) has the same closed-polynomial
consequence for \(5S_4(x,y,1)-rP_4(x,y,1)\), with \(r\)-degree at
most two. Reduction modulo \(r\) and the degree bounds give
\[
             S_4=TL+\delta z^4,\qquad P_4=\alpha zT+5z^3L+\beta z^4
 \tag{5}
\]
for a binary linear \(L\) and constants \(\alpha,\beta,\delta\).
Subtract \(\alpha R\) from \(P\). In the degree-nine equation of (3),
insert (5) and put \(z=1\). In this chart let \(l=L(x,y)\),
\(p=P_3(x,y,1)\), \(s=S_3(x,y,1)\) and \(r_3=R_3(x,y,1)\).
The equation is
\[
  \big\{5s-5lr_3-rp+(10l^2+4\beta l)r-20\delta l,\ r\big\}=0 .
\]
For example, the terms involving \(R_3\) before setting \(z=1\)
sum to \(5z^4\{T,LR_3\}\), while the terms quadratic in \(P_4,S_4\)
or from their \(z\) derivatives contribute
\((20z^3LT+4\beta z^4T-20\delta z^7)\{L,T\}\).
The expression in braces is polynomial in \(r\) of \(r\)-degree
at most two. Applying the degree bounds after reduction modulo \(r\)
gives
\[
                     P_3=\gamma T+10zL^2+z^2L_1+\epsilon z^3
\]
for a binary linear \(L_1\) and constants \(\gamma,\epsilon\).

Take weights \((2,2,1)\) and put \(T_0=T(x,y,0)\ne0\).
The top terms of \(R,Q\) are \(zT_0,\ (z^2+L)T_0\).
If \(\gamma\ne0\), the top of \(P\) is \(\gamma T_0\), and the
determinant of these three weighted forms is a nonzero scalar
times \(T_0^2\{L,T_0\}\). If \(\gamma=0\), the top of \(P\) is
\(z^5+5z^3L+10zL^2\). Computing the determinant in \(L,T_0,z\)
gives \(30L^2 T_0 z\), so its pullback in the original variables
vanishes only if \(L=0\) or \(\{L,T_0\}=0\). Thus in either case
\(L=0\) or \(T_0\) is a nonzero multiple of \(L^3\).
The next argument settles the \(L=0\) alternative.

### Vanishing binary linear term

Suppose \(L=0\). The degree-nine equation, reduced modulo \(r\),
makes \(p,s\) affine linear in \(r\), since their binary degrees
are at most three. Thus \(P_3=aT+\text{a multiple of }z^3\)
and \(S_3=bT+\text{a multiple of }z^3\).

If \(a=0\), then \(P=f(z)+P_{\le2}\), with \(f\) monic of degree five.
Such a polynomial with no critical point admits a coordinate
completion of inverse degree at most five. To see this, the
quadratic part in the binary variables has rank zero, one or two.
At rank two, solving \(P_x=P_y=0\) makes \(x,y\) affine in \(z\),
and the remaining \(P_z\) is a nonconstant quartic. At rank one,
normalize that part to \(x^2\). If the affine coefficient in
\(z\) of \(y\) is nonconstant, solve at its root for \(x\) and
then for \(y\) in the \(z\) derivative. If it vanishes identically,
solving \(P_x=0\) again gives a nonconstant quartic in \(z\).
Thus that coefficient must be a nonzero constant, which gives the
graph completion. At rank zero write \(P=f(z)+a_0(z)x+b_0(z)y\)
with affine \(a_0,b_0\). A common zero of these coefficients would
give a critical point by solving the \(z\) derivative, or \(P\)
would be univariate. Therefore a constant linear combination of
\(a_0,b_0\) equals one. Complete by an independent linear binary
coordinate using this Bézout identity. The inverse is linear in
the two fibre coordinates with affine coefficients in \(z\),
plus a constant multiple of \(f(z)\), so its degree is at most
five. The [bounded coordinate-completion theorem](LOW_DEGREE_COORDINATE_KELLER_MAPS_IN_THREE_VARIABLES.md)
now gives invertibility of \(F\).

Assume \(a\ne0\). In the degree-eight equation of (3), denote the
binary dehomogenizations of \(S_2,P_2\) by \(s_2,p_2\).
The equation is
\[
                  \{5s_2-rp_2+(a r-5b)r_3,\ r\}=0 .
 \tag{6}
\]
Indeed the only terms of that degree are those from
\(5z^4(\{S_2,zT\}+\{bT,R_3\})\) and
\(-zT(\{P_2,zT\}+\{aT,R_3\})\).
Since the expression inside the bracket lies in \(\mathbb C[r]\)
and has degree at most six, its degree-six terms give that the
degree-three binary part of \(r_3\) is proportional to \(T_0\).
Translate \(z\) to eliminate this part of \(R_3\), using that the
translation contribution from \(R_4=zT\) is a multiple of \(T+zT_z\).
The translation preserves the pure \(z^4\) terms of \(P,S\),
after subtracting a constant multiple of \(R\) from \(Q\) to
maintain the substitution \(S=Q-zR\).
Thus \(z\mid R_3\) in the new coordinates.

Now \(r_3,s_2,p_2\) have binary degrees at most two.
Reduction of (6) modulo \(r\), and then division by \(r\),
gives \(P_2=a R_3/z+\text{a multiple of }z^2\) and
\(S_2=b R_3/z+\text{a multiple of }z^2\).
Writing \(U=T+R_3/z\), we obtain
\[
       P=f(z)+aU+P_1,\qquad S=g_0(z)+bU+S_1,\qquad
       R=zU+R_2+R_1
\]
after discarding constants, which do not affect the argument
(the constant of \(R\) can be absorbed in the univariate part
of \(S\)). Subtract \(bP/a\) from \(Q\), so
\(S=g(z)+L_{\rm aff}\) with an affine linear \(L_{\rm aff}\).
The polynomial \(K=R-zP/a\) has \(x,y\)-dependent part of degree
at most two. Consequently \(\{P,R\}=\{P,K\}\) has degree at most
three. If \(L_{\rm aff}\) is independent of \(x,y\), absorb it
in \(g\). Then (3) gives \(\kappa=-(R+g')\{P,R\}\), impossible
since \(R+g'\) is nonconstant. Otherwise use its binary linear part
as the coordinate \(x\), and again absorb any \(z\) term and constant
in \(g\). The exact equation is now
\[
                   \kappa=-(R+g')\{P,R\}+P_zR_y-P_yR_z .
\]
Its degree-seven part, divided by \(z\), gives
\[
                 (T+\xi z^3)\{aT,K_2\}=5z^4 T_y
\]
for a constant \(\xi\), where \(K_2\) may be taken to be the
degree-two part of the \(x,y\)-dependent part of \(K\).
The degree-three polynomial \(T+\xi z^3\) is coprime to \(z\)
and hence divides \(T_y\), which has degree at most two.
Therefore \(T_y=0\), making \(T\) a binary cubic and contradicting
its irreducibility. This excludes \(a\ne0\).
The remaining cubic-pencil case has \(L\ne0,\ T_0\propto L^3\).

The [cubic-pencil closure](QUINTIC_KELLER_RANK_TWO_CUBIC_PENCIL_CLOSURE.md)
proves invertibility in the remaining cubic-plus-line cases.

## Two reduced conics

Suppose \(C=UV\) with distinct irreducible quadrics \(U,V\).
Equation (2) forces \(m\ge2\).
For \(m=5\) the polynomial \(g\) is constant. A fibre containing
\(U\) must be supported on \(U,V\) with equal multiplicities by (2),
so its degree would be even. For \(m=3\), the left side at \(U\)
is positive since \(\deg g=2\). Hence all factors of that member
must be in \(C\), again making the degree even. Both are impossible.

For \(m=4\), the linear \(g=\ell\) has its own member \(\ell^4\)
because its left side in (2) is negative while those at \(U,V\)
are positive. If \(U,V\) occur in distinct members, those members
must be \(U^2,V^2\). But a linear combination of \(U^2,V^2\)
cannot be \(\ell^4\): the two quadratic factors of a combination
using both squares would both have to be multiples of \(\ell^2\).
Thus \(C=UV\) is a single member and the leading span is
\(\langle\ell^5,\ell C\rangle\). By (3)–(4) this is impossible
unless \(C|_{\ell=1}\) is composite. In that situation the
dehomogenized factors \(U|_{\ell=1},V|_{\ell=1}\) are distinct
level polynomials of the same quadratic, since they are
irreducible quadrics. Homogenizing their affine relation puts
\(\ell^2\) in the pencil spanned by \(U,V\).

For \(m=2\), both \(U,V\) are whole pencil members and span it.
By (1), \(\deg g\) is one or three. In any reduced member, either
that member is \(U\) or \(V\), or its factors occur with equal
multiplicities in \(g\) by (2). Each such member therefore
contributes an even degree to \(g\). Its odd degree forces
a nonreduced member, necessarily the square of a line.
Thus again the conics \(U,V\) span a pencil containing a double
line \(\ell^2\).

Write \(U=q+a\ell^2,\ V=q+b\ell^2\) up to scalars, with \(a\ne b\).
The rank of \(q|_{\ell=0}\) is positive because \(U,V\) are
irreducible. At rank two the conics meet at two distinct points
on \(\ell=0\), transverse to that line. In local coordinates
\((q,\ell)\) they have equations \(q+a\ell^2=q+b\ell^2=0\),
respectively, so the two singularities of their union are ordinary
tacnodes. This contradicts the quartic tacnode exclusion.
At rank one choose \(\ell=z\) and reduce \(q\) to \(x^2+yz\)
up to a multiple of \(z^2\). Indeed complete the square in \(x\);
the \(yz\) coefficient is nonzero or both conics would factor.
Shift \(y\) by a multiple of \(z\) to make \(a=0\), and rescale
to make \(b=1\). The intersection is a single point at \(x=z=0\)
with contact order four, proving the claimed necessary form.

The [contact-four exclusion](KELLER_QUARTIC_CONTACT_FOUR_CONIC_EXCLUSION.md)
rules out this contact configuration as a quartic Keller component.
Together with the other boundary and factor exclusions, it gives
invertibility in top rank two with a reduced leading quartic.
