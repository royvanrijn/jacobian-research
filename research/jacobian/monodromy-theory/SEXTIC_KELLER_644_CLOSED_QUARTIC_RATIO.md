# The closed quartic ratio in profile (6,4,4)

**Theorem SexticKeller644ClosedQuarticRatioLinearSquareReduction.**
Let \(F\) be a characteristic-zero three-variable Keller map of
ordinary component degrees \((6,4,4)\) with highest tuple \(A,B,C\).
Suppose its two quartic highest forms are coprime and squarefree
over the algebraic closure, and \(B/C\) is a closed parameter in
the projective plane function field over the algebraic closure.
Then its leading tuple, after a linear change on the lower two
components and a linear source change over the algebraic closure,
has form
\[
                  (a x^6+b x^2 C,\ x^4,\ C).
\]
The quartic \(C/x^4\) remains a closed parameter.

Work over the algebraic closure \(k\). Put \(L=k(\mathbb P^2)\).
As in the [mixed-degree reduction](SEXTIC_KELLER_MIXED_DEGREE_LEADING_REDUCTIONS.md#the-equal-degree-lower-pencils),
the highest determinant forces \(A\) algebraic over \(k(B,C)\).
Then \(A^2/C^3\), of homogeneous degree zero, is algebraic over
\(k(B/C)\): otherwise \(B/C,A^2/C^3\) have transcendence degree
two and adjoining the independent radial form \(C\) raises it
to three. Closedness therefore puts \(\eta=A^2/C^3\) in \(k(B/C)\).

The [parity argument for the binary case](SEXTIC_KELLER_644_BINARY_NORMALIZATION.md#projective-index-four)
applies to factorizations of the ternary pencil as well.
The place \(C=0\) on its parameter line has odd order of
\(\eta\) because \(C\) is squarefree. Every other odd place
has a square member: the order on any irreducible factor
of that member is even and equals the product of the
order on the parameter line and the factor's multiplicity.
There are at most three square members. Indeed, in a pencil
containing two independent squares \(K_1^2,K_2^2\), any
third square factors as a product of coprime quadratics in
\(\langle K_1,K_2\rangle\), each of which must be a square.
A pencil of quadratics has at most two square members,
since a pencil containing two independent squares of
linear forms has rank two everywhere off those two members.
The two quadratic squares determine the third quartic
member up to scalar. Moreover, if there are three square
quartics, the entire quadratic pencil is in the two linear
forms obtained this way. Then the quartic ratio is binary,
of degree four as a rational function on the binary
projective line by coprimality. That binary coordinate is
algebraic of degree four over the ratio, contradicting
closedness in \(L\).

It follows that there is exactly one other odd place.
Call its member \(K^2\). Retain \(C\) and use
\(R=K^2/C\) as parameter. Then \(\eta/R\) has even
orders throughout the parameter line, so it is a square
in \(k(R)\). Since \(u=A/C\) satisfies
\((u/K)^2=\eta/R\), we obtain \(k(A,B,C)=k(K,C)\).
The pair \((K,C)\) is onto. For prescribed nonzero values,
the corresponding projective pencil member has a point
off \(KC=0\), because its defining polynomial is coprime
to both \(K\) and \(C\). Rescaling that point fixes the
value of \(K\) and thereby the value of \(C\).
One zero value is obtained on the corresponding divisor
away from the other, and the origin gives \((0,0)\).
Polynomial contraction therefore gives \(A=aK^3+bKC\).

Suppose \(K\) is squarefree. Then \(k(K,C)\) is relatively
algebraically closed in \(k(x,y,z)\). To see this, use a linear
radial coordinate \(\ell\) not vanishing on any component of
\(K=0\), and put \(s_0=K/\ell^2\in L\). In the source function
field, \(K\) is transcendental over \(L\), and \(L(\ell)\) is
the quadratic extension of \(M=L(K)\) obtained by adjoining
\(\sqrt{K/s_0}\). As \(R\) is closed and the characteristic is
zero, \(M\) is regular over \(k(R,K)\), by adjoining an independent
variable to the regular extension \(L/k(R)\). If \(k(R,K)\)
were not relatively closed in \(L(\ell)\), it would have
a quadratic subextension there. Indeed, a finite algebraic
extension generated by a new element is linearly disjoint
from \(M\) over \(k(R,K)\), so its degree is at most two.
Writing it as \(k(R,K)(\sqrt{d})\) would force
\(d/(K/s_0)\) to be a square in \(M\).

Use the divisorial valuation of a projective component of
\(K=0\), extended to \(M\) by giving the independent variable
\(K\) its Gauss valuation zero. On \(L\) this divisor has
order two on \(R\) and order one on \(s_0\). Every nonzero
element of \(k(R)\) has even valuation, twice its order at
\(R=0\). The Gauss construction therefore also gives even
valuation to every nonzero element of \(k(R,K)\). Hence \(d\)
has even valuation whereas \(K/s_0\) has odd valuation,
a contradiction. This proves relative closedness.

Surjectivity gives the polynomial kernel \(k[K,C]\) for
the derivation \(\det J(K,C,\cdot)\). If \(b\ne0\), the
minors of the leading tuple as a function of \(K,C\)
include \(2K\) and \(3aK^2+bC\), which are coprime.
The [saturation theorem](SEXTIC_KELLER_MIXED_DEGREE_LEADING_REDUCTIONS.md)
excludes a Keller completion. If \(b=0\), rescale the sextic
to obtain the weighted cusp \((K^2,K^3,C)\) in a reordered
frame with ordinary source weights. Its expected determinant
degree is eleven, and \(K,C\) are coprime, onto, and have
relatively closed field. The
[squarefree weighted cusp exclusion](SEXTIC_KELLER_ALIGNED_CUSP_EXCLUSION.md)
applies. Thus \(K\) cannot be squarefree. A nonzero
quadratic over the algebraic closure that is not squarefree
is a scalar multiple of a square of a linear form. This
gives the displayed linear-square tuple. The new ratio
\(C/x^4\) is a fractional linear transform of the original
parameter and is therefore closed.

**Corollary SexticKeller644ClosedQuarticRatioPurePowersReduction.**
Under the hypotheses of SexticKeller644ClosedQuarticRatioLinearSquareReduction,
if \(F\) is noninvertible then its highest tuple in the stated
frame has form \((x^6,x^4,C)\), after a scalar change.

Write \(P,Q,T\) for the components in degrees \(4,6,4\)
in the linear-square frame, with highest forms \(x^4\),
\(a x^6+b x^2 C\), \(C\). Suppose \(b\ne0\). The pair
\((x,C)\) has relatively closed field, since \(C/x^4\) is
a closed projective parameter and adjoining the independent
radial variable \(x\) preserves regularity. It is onto
since \(h=C(0,y,z)\ne0\) is the highest transverse
part for every fixed \(x\).

Use ordinary rescaling of the components with \(\epsilon\),
so the determinant is \(\kappa\epsilon^{11}\).
As in the [aligned root subtraction](SEXTIC_KELLER_ALIGNED_LINEAR_POWERS.md#formal-expansion-at-the-first-weight),
set \(X=P_\epsilon^{1/4}=x+O(\epsilon)\). Then
\[
 Q_\epsilon\equiv\sum_{0\le i<11}\epsilon^i D_i(X,T_\epsilon)
                   \pmod{\epsilon^{11}},
 \qquad D_0=aX^6+bX^2T_\epsilon .
 \tag{1}
\]
At each unmatched order the residual coefficient is in the
rational kernel of \(\det J(x,C,\cdot)\) by the determinant.
It is polynomial after localizing at \(x\), so onto
contraction puts it in \(k[x,x^{-1},C]\).
Its ordinary degree under scaling is \(6-i\), so the terms
in \(D_i\) are scalar multiples of \(X^{6-i-4j}T_\epsilon^j\)
for integers \(j\ge0\). Correcting by these expressions
raises the error order without changing the bracket with
\(P_\epsilon,T_\epsilon\), proving (1).

Decrease the weight of \(x\), keeping the weights of \(y,z\)
one, to the first tie in \(P\) with \(x^4\) or in \(Q\)
with \(b x^2h\). There is a tie since \(P\) cannot be
univariate in \(x\): otherwise its degree-four derivative
would vanish somewhere, contradicting the submersion
condition. The tie weight \(v\) is in
\(\{1/4,1/3,1/2,2/3,3/4\}\). For \(P\) this follows
from the ratios \((j+l)/(4-i)\) for its transverse
monomials \(x^i y^j z^l\) below ordinary degree four.
For \(Q\), all ordinary degree-six terms other than
\(b x^2h\) have \(x\)-degree at least three and hence
lower weight once \(v<1\). A lower ordinary term with
\(x\)-degree one has transverse degree at most four
and cannot tie. A pure transverse term can tie only
if it has degree five and \(v=1/2\).
The highest term of \(T\) at the decreased weight is \(h\).

Use minimum weights \(1-v,1,0,0\) in \(x,\epsilon,y,z\).
Then the minimum weights of \(P_\epsilon,Q_\epsilon,T_\epsilon\)
are \(4(1-v),2(1-v),0\). A nonzero term
\(\epsilon^i X^{6-i-4j}T_\epsilon^j\) of (1) must have
minimum weight at least \(2(1-v)\), since at each new
coefficient order in \(\epsilon\) the distinct \(j\) have
distinct weights and earlier subtractions have no terms
of smaller weight. At equality it satisfies
\[
                         i v=4(1-v)(j-1).
\]
For \(v=p/q\) in lowest terms, equality with positive \(i\)
first occurs at \(i=4(q-p)/\gcd(p,4)\). If \(v\ne1/2\),
this is strictly greater than \(q-p\). But the tie then
occurs in \(P\), and its first added term in the minimum
part of \(P_\epsilon\) has order \(q-p\) in \(\epsilon\)
by the five listed fractions. In the minimum part of
\(bX^2T_\epsilon\) it gives a nonzero term at that order,
from the linear term of the square root. The minimum
part of \(Q_\epsilon\) is pure \(b x^2h\) and the weight
of \(aX^6\) is greater than \(2(1-v)\), so this contradicts
(1). All comparisons occur strictly below order eleven.

Thus \(v=1/2\). With \(\tau=\epsilon/x^2\) the two
minimum parts are \(x^4(1+L\tau+M\tau^2)\) and
\(x^2(bh+S_5\tau)\), for transverse homogeneous forms
\(L,M,S_5\) of degrees one, two, and five.
The positive-order equalities from the correction terms
in (1) start at order four. Therefore the \(\tau^2\)
coefficient of the minimum part of \(bX^2T_\epsilon\)
must vanish, giving \(M=L^2/4\), since
\([\,\tau^2\,](1+L\tau+M\tau^2)^{1/2}=M/2-L^2/8\).
If \(L=0\), then \(M=0\) and (1) also gives \(S_5=0\),
contrary to the tie. In weights \((1,2,2)\) the highest
quartic form of \(P\) is therefore \((x^2+L/2)^2\)
with \(L\ne0\).
The [weighted-square coordinate theorem](SEXTIC_KELLER_ALIGNED_CUSP_EXCLUSION.md)
gives inversion. It follows that noninvertibility in the
linear-square frame requires \(b=0\), and scaling \(Q\)
gives the claimed pure powers.


## Inversion for the closed quartic ratio

**Theorem SexticKeller644AlignedLinearPowersInversion.**
Let \((P,Q,T)\) be a characteristic-zero three-variable Keller map
with ordinary highest forms \(x^4,x^6,C\), respectively, where
\(C\) has degree four, \(h=C(0,y,z)\ne0\), and \(C/x^4\) is a
closed projective parameter over the algebraic closure.
Then \(P\) has a polynomial coordinate completion with inverse
degree at most four, and the map is invertible.

Work over the algebraic closure \(k\). As in the preceding proofs,
\(k(x,C)\) is relatively algebraically closed in \(k(x,y,z)\),
and \((x,C)\) is onto. In particular the polynomial kernel
of \(\det J(x,C,\cdot)\) is \(k[x,C]\). The same formal
root subtraction as in (1) therefore gives
\[
 Q_\epsilon\equiv
   \sum_{0\le i<11,\;j\ge0}
      c_{ij}\epsilon^i X^{6-i-4j}T_\epsilon^j
       \pmod{\epsilon^{11}},
 \qquad X=P_\epsilon^{1/4}=x+O(\epsilon).
 \tag{2}
\]
For clarity, the rescaled determinant is a nonzero scalar times
\(\epsilon^{11}\). At the first unmatched order, its coefficient
equation places the residual in \(k(x,C)\).
The residual is polynomial after localizing at \(x\), so the
onto contraction gives \(k[x,x^{-1},C]\).
Homogeneity of degree \(6-i\) gives the exponents in (2).
Lifting the invariant coefficient from \(x,C\) to
\(X,T_\epsilon\) raises the unmatched order.
This uses the [formal root subtraction for aligned powers](SEXTIC_KELLER_ALIGNED_LINEAR_POWERS.md#formal-expansion-at-the-first-weight),
adapted here to a quartic third component.
The ordinary highest sextic gives \(c_{00}=1\) and \(c_{0j}=0\)
for \(j>0\). Subtract scalar multiples of \(P,T,1\) from \(Q\)
to set \(c_{20}=c_{21}=c_{60}=0\). Write \(\gamma=c_{11}\).

### Lowest weight and the mixed correction

Since \(P\) is a submersion of degree four, it depends on \(y,z\).
Decrease the weight of \(x\) from one to the first tie in \(P\)
with \(x^4\), keeping the other two weights one.
Write this weight as \(w=r/s\) in lowest terms. The possibilities
\((r,s)\) are \((1,2),(1,3),(2,3),(1,4),(3,4)\):
a transverse monomial \(x^u y^v z^b\) of lower ordinary degree
ties at the ratio of its transverse degree to \(4-u\).
Put \(a=1-r/s\), and take minimum weights \(a,1,0,0\)
in \(x,\epsilon,y,z\). With the formal variable
\(t=\epsilon^{s-r}/x^s\) the minimum parts are
\[
 P_{\epsilon,*}=x^4 f(t),\qquad T_{\epsilon,*}=h,\qquad
 f(0)=1,\quad 1\le\deg_t f\le\lfloor 4/s\rfloor.
\]
The coefficient of \(t^n\) in \(f\) is homogeneous of transverse
degree \(nr\).

The term \(c_{ij}\epsilon^i X^v T_\epsilon^j\) of (2), with
\(v=6-i-4j\), has minimum weight
\[
                       6a+ i(r/s)-4ja.
\]
For any least weight among nonzero terms in (2), take the
smallest \(\epsilon\)-index \(i\) attaining that weight.
There is only one such \(j\) at that \(i\), and the
\(\epsilon^i\)-coefficient of the least-weight part is
\(c_{ij}x^v h^j\). It cannot have \(v<0\), since the left
side of (2) is polynomial and \(i<11\). This proves that
the least weight is bounded below by the smallest weight
of a nonzero term with \(v\ge0\).
The only possibilities for \(v\ge0\) after the target
subtractions are \(j=0\) and \((i,j)=(1,1)\).

If \(\gamma\ne0\), the weight of \(\epsilon X T_\epsilon\)
is below \(6a\), since \(r/s\le3/4<4/5\).
It is then the least weight. The other indices at that
weight have
\(i=1+n(s-r),\ j=1+nr/4\) with \(n>0,\ 4\mid nr\).
The contribution from \((1,1)\) is
\(\epsilon x h\gamma f(t)^{1/4}\).
For \(1\le n\le\lfloor4/s\rfloor\), no other indices
contribute at its \(\epsilon^{1+n(s-r)}\)-coefficient,
since \(0<nr<4\). These indices are all below eleven,
and \(1-ns<0\). Polynomiality requires the corresponding
coefficients in \(f^{1/4}\) to vanish. This makes \(f=1\)
to its full degree, contrary to the tie. Hence \(\gamma=0\).

### The weighted square

Now the least weight in (2) is \(6a\).
The terms at equality have
\(i=n(s-r),\ j=nr/4\) with \(n\ge0,\ 4\mid nr\).
Division by \(x^6\) gives the following series for that
weight:
\[
 f(t)^{3/2}+
 \sum_{\substack{n>0,\;4\mid nr\\ n(s-r)<11}}
      d_n\,h^{nr/4}t^n f(t)^{(6-ns)/4}.
 \tag{3}
\]
A coefficient of \(t^n\) must vanish if \(ns>6\) and
\(n(s-r)<11\), again by polynomiality in \(x\).
No higher-weight part of (2) enters these equations.

For \(s=4\), \(f=1+L t\) and the correction terms in
(3) start at \(n=4\). Its coefficient at \(n=2\) is
\(3L^2/8\), so \(L=0\), impossible.
For \(s=3\), \(f=1+L t\) again. For \(r=1\) the
corrections start at \(n=4\). For \(r=2\) the first
one is a multiple of \(h t^2\), since \(6-2s=0\),
and the next starts at \(n=4\). Thus at \(n=3\) both
cases give \(-L^3/16=0\), a contradiction.

It remains that \(r=1,\ s=2\). Write \(f=1+L t+M t^2\).
Through order six the series (3) is \(f^{3/2}+D t^4 f^{-1/2}\),
where \(D=d_4 h\).
At \(n=4\) the vanishing gives
\(D=-3(L^2-4M)^2/128\).
With this substitution the coefficient at \(n=6\) is
\(- (L^2-4M)^3/512\). Both computations are reproduced by
the [exact coefficient checker](check_sextic_644_closed_quartic_ratio.py).
The vanishing at \(n=6\) gives \(M=L^2/4\).
The nontrivial tie implies \(L\ne0\). Thus the highest
form of \(P\) in weights \((1,2,2)\) is
\((x^2+L/2)^2\), with \(L\) a nonzero linear form in the
transverse variables. The
[weighted-square coordinate theorem](SEXTIC_KELLER_ALIGNED_CUSP_EXCLUSION.md)
gives the bounded completion and inversion. Inversion descends
by uniqueness of the formal inverse.

**Corollary SexticKeller644ClosedQuarticRatioInversion.**
Let \(F\) be a characteristic-zero three-variable Keller map
of ordinary component degrees \((6,4,4)\).
If the two quartic highest forms are coprime and their
projective ratio is closed over the algebraic closure, then
\(F\) is invertible.

Indeed one can choose two independent squarefree members of
the lower pencil by a constant target change over the
algebraic closure. On the complement of the base locus the
pencil gives a nonconstant projective morphism to a line, so
its generic fibre is generically smooth in characteristic zero
and reduced. No component of a member is contained in the
base locus since the generators are coprime. Equivalently,
a repeated component in a finite fibre lies in the critical
locus of the ratio and is therefore one of finitely many
irreducible divisors. Thus squarefree members form a nonempty
open subset of the parameter line. Closedness is unchanged
by a fractional linear transform of the ratio. The pure-powers
reduction and the preceding theorem apply, and the inverse
descends to the original field.
