# A degree-growth obstruction for uniform-slope private-unit common factors

Consider an ordinary two-monomial profile with one private unit
row in each orientation and arbitrary shared rows. At two
consecutive orders \(n,N=n+1\) suppose a common monic irreducible
rational factor \(H\) has degree \(d\). Assume that at each
rational prime all roots of \(H\) have the same valuation.
The valuation hypothesis applies to \(H\), so it can follow
from a degree-bound and shared Newton slopes even if the full
moment polynomials have several slopes.

**Theorem.** For \(N\ge7\) and \(N\ge132d(d-2)\), the only
possible field of a root of \(H\) is
\(\mathbb Q(\zeta_4)\), \(\mathbb Q(\zeta_6)\), or
\(\mathbb Q(\zeta_{12})\), with the corresponding angular
congruence in the [CM-field table](MULTIVARIATE_CM_COMMON_FACTOR_GATE.md).
The common slope valuation must be a half-integer outside
\(\mathbb Z\) at \(2\) for order four, at \(3\) for order six,
and at both \(2\) and \(3\) for order twelve.
There is no curvature restriction or assumption that the field
is CM. If the three field cases fail, every uniform-slope
common factor at \(N\ge7\) must satisfy
\(132d(d-2)>N\). In particular an a priori small degree bound
can be combined with this obstruction without a large-order
coefficient scan.

## Narrow radius and an algebraic unit

At shared absolute curvature at least \(15/8\), the
[absolute curvature theorem](MULTIVARIATE_BOTH_PRIVATE_UNIT_CURVATURE_15_OVER_8.md)
already excludes a common root. Otherwise the
[absolute radial wall](MULTIVARIATE_PRIVATE_UNIT_IMBALANCE_TWENTY_FIVE.md)
puts all endpoint-normalized order-\(N\) roots at log modulus
between \(-11/N\) and \(11/N\). Hence the logarithms of the
absolute values of the physical roots of \(H\) have diameter
at most \(22/N\). The diameter is invariant under the
positive normalization scale.

Write \(c\) for the product of the roots of \(H\), a positive
rational since the roots are all nonreal and conjugate paired.
For one root \(\alpha\) put \(u=\alpha^d/c\).
The equal-valuation hypothesis implies \(v_p(u)=0\) at every
finite place, since \(v_p(c)\) is \(d\) times the shared
valuation. Thus \(u\) is an algebraic unit of degree at most
\(d\). Choose a root of \(H\) of maximal modulus \(R\).
Its conjugate also contributes that maximal modulus. Therefore
the log house of \(u\), the logarithm of the maximum of the
absolute values of its conjugates, is at most
\(22(d-2)/N\).

By the [Schinzel–Zassenhaus bound](https://annals.math.princeton.edu/articles/22975),
if \(u\) is not a root of unity, its log house is at least
\(\log 2/(4d)\). This exceeds \(1/(6d)\), since
\(\int_0^1(1+x)^{-1}dx>2/3\) by strict convexity.
Under the stated order hypothesis the log house is at most
\(1/(6d)\) when \(d>2\), and is zero when \(d=2\).
Thus \(u\) is a root of unity and all roots of \(H\) have
equal moduli. All their ratios are also roots of unity.

## Reduction to the cyclotomic table

Let \(L\) be the splitting field of \(H\) and \(L_0\) the
field generated by all its root ratios. The latter is
cyclotomic. Any automorphism of \(L\) over \(L_0\) acts on
the roots by a common scalar, since it fixes each ratio.
The sum of the roots is rational and strictly positive
because all the roots have positive real part by the
[both-private angle boxes](MULTIVARIATE_PRIVATE_UNIT_ASYMMETRIC_ANGLE_BOXES.md).
Hence the scalar is one and \(L=L_0\).
The splitting field is abelian, so it equals each root
field. This makes the CM gate applicable.

Its possible order \(10\) is incompatible with equality of
the moduli. In that case
\(\alpha=(1+q)t\), with \(q\) of order ten and
\(t\in\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)\). Because
\(\mathrm{Nm}(1+q)=5\), the norm of \(\alpha\) is five times
a rational square. But it also equals \(R^4\), a rational
square since the equal products \(\alpha\bar\alpha=R^2\)
are fixed by the full Galois group. This is a contradiction.
For order \(12\), the same equality of moduli gives
\[
    R^2=t^2(2+q+q^{-1})
                 =\frac{((1\pm\sqrt3)t)^2}{2}.
\]
The square of \(y=(1\pm\sqrt3)t\) is rational, so \(y\) is
rational or a rational multiple of \(\sqrt3\). Since \(t\)
is totally positive, the signs of \(y\) at the two real
embeddings are opposite. Thus \(y\) is a rational multiple
of \(\sqrt3\), giving \(R^2=3r^2/2\) for some \(r>0\) rational.
Both \(v_2(R^2)\) and \(v_3(R^2)\) are odd. At each of these
primes the roots of \(H\) have uniform valuation and
\(\alpha\bar\alpha=R^2\), giving the half-integer restrictions.

Choosing the conjugate with \(q=e^{2\pi i/12}\), the equality
above gives \(\alpha=r[3+\sqrt3+i(3-\sqrt3)]/4\).
Consequently the only order-twelve common factor under these
conditions is of the form
\[
        z^4-3r z^3+\tfrac92 r^2z^2-\tfrac92 r^3z+\tfrac94 r^4 .
\]
One can check this directly using the two quadratic factors
of constant term \(R^2\), with traces
\(r(3+\sqrt3)/2\) and \(r(3-\sqrt3)/2\).
At \(p=2\) its monic stepped residual polynomial is
\(X^2+X+1\), and at \(p=3\) it is \(X^2+1\).
Indeed the slope denominator is two and the reduced square
\(\alpha^2/p^a\), \(a=v_p(R^2)\), differs from the reduction
of \(q\) by a rational unit. These reductions of \(q\)
have orders three and four, respectively. Multiplication
by a unit modulo two, or by either sign modulo three,
preserves the asserted residual polynomial.

The order \(4,6\) conditions follow directly
from the CM table. This completes the exclusion.
