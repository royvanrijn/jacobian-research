# The surviving marker at a quadruple leading root

Use the regular phase \(f=x^{-1}(C+Bt+Dt^2+Et^3)\) and the normalized
sphere functional \(\Lambda\) of the
[logarithmic boundary theorem](WINDING_ONE_CUBIC_LOGARITHMIC_BOUNDARY.md).
The field has characteristic zero. Suppose that the leading coefficient
has a quadruple root \(r\ne0\) and is either of degree four or of degree
five with one further simple root \(s\ne0,r\).

**Proposition.** If \(f\) is eventually pure, the fixed marker \(x\)
satisfies \(\Lambda(xf^m)\ne0\) for infinitely many \(m\). In the
degree-five case the ratio \(\mu=r/s\) is algebraic over \(\mathbb Q\)
and cannot equal \(-1\). For any fixed degree bound and starting order
of the pure tail, the possible ratios belong to a finite set of
algebraic numbers.

Thus in both remaining horizontal septic cases, existence of even an
eventually pure phase would provide a fixed surviving marker. The
proposition is a necessary condition on the remaining parameters.

## The unmarked and marked coefficients

At a sufficiently large good prime \(p=3k+1\), the maximum height in
\(f^k\) or \(xf^k\) is \(p-1\). This height is even and it is the
only height with interval denominator divisible by \(p\). Therefore
\[
 p\Lambda(f^k)\equiv[x^k]E(x)^k,\qquad
 p\Lambda(xf^k)\equiv[x^{k-1}]E(x)^k.                     \tag{1}
\]
These identities use coefficient reduction in any residue field of
characteristic \(p\) in which the coefficient data are integral and
the root parameters are units. They require no assumption about
absolute residue degree. The factor \(p\) clears the denominators at
height \(p-1\), and all other denominators are units at \(p\).

Normalize by \(x=rX\) and divide \(E(rX)\) by its nonzero constant
coefficient. This changes the two coefficients in (1) by units at
good primes. In degree four the normalized polynomial is
\((1-X)^4\), so
\[
 (1-X)^{4k}\equiv(1-X^p)(1-X)^{k-1}\pmod p.
\]
The coefficient at \(X^k\) is zero and the coefficient at \(X^{k-1}\)
is a unit. In degree five the extra factor is \(1-\mu X\). Put
\[
 S(X)=(1-X)^{k-1}(1-\mu X)^k,\quad
 A=[X^k]S(X),\quad L=[X^{k-1}]S(X).
\]
Equation (1), up to the units, gives \(A,L\) in the two positions.
Introduce the polynomial
\[
 H(\mu)=[X^k](1-X)S(X)=(-1)^k\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}^2\mu^j.
\]
Comparing coefficients using
\(\binom{k-1}{k-j}=(j/k)\binom{k}{j}\) gives
\[
                  A=\frac{\mu}{k}H'(\mu),\qquad L=A-H(\mu).
\]
In characteristic \(p\), coefficient comparison also gives
\[
 \mu(1-\mu)H''+(1+(2k-1)\mu)H'-k^2H=0 .                 \tag{2}
\]
Indeed the consecutive coefficients satisfy
\((j+1)^2 H_{j+1}=(k-j)^2 H_j\). Away from \(0,1\), a common
zero of \(H,H'\) would force every Taylor coefficient to vanish by
(2), since \(\deg H=k<p\) and all the integers in the recurrence
up to order \(k\) are units. Since \(H(0)=(-1)^k\), such a zero is
impossible. Consequently \(A=0\) implies \(L=-H(\mu)\ne0\).

For a given characteristic-zero phase choose one number-field
specialization preserving the unit coefficient data and distinct
roots. To make it, pass to a maximal ideal of the finitely generated
\(\mathbb Q\)-algebra generated by the coefficients, root parameters
and the inverses of the required nonzero elements. Every moment is
a rational polynomial in the phase coefficients, so a vanishing tail
survives. The coefficients of the specialization are integral with
the specified parameters units at all but finitely many rational
primes. At every remaining \(p\equiv1\pmod3\), equation (1) forces
\(A=0\) in the degree-five case. The conclusion on \(L\) (and the
binomial coefficient in degree four) proves the claimed marked
nonvanishing in the specialization and hence in the original field.

## Constraints on the degree-five ratio

At \(\mu=-1\), the integer \(k\) is even for odd \(p=3k+1\), and
\(H(-1)=(-1)^{k/2}\binom{k}{k/2}\) by the coefficient of \(X^k\)
in \((1-X^2)^k\). This is nonzero modulo \(p\). The reciprocal
identity \(H(\mu)=\mu^k H(1/\mu)\) gives
\(2H'(-1)=-kH(-1)\). Therefore \(A\ne0\), contradicting eventual
purity by (1).

For algebraicity, suppose \(\mu\) were transcendental. Choose a
finitely generated \(\mathbb Z\)-subalgebra of the field containing
the phase and root coefficients and inverses of the required units.
Its spectrum maps dominantly to \(\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb Z[\mu]\).
Chevalley's theorem gives a nonempty principal open \(D(g)\) in
the image. For arbitrarily large primes \(p=3k+1\) the reduction of
\(g\) is nonzero. Choose a value of \(\mu\) in the algebraic closure
of \(\mathbb F_p\) outside the zeros of \(g\), \(H'\), \(\mu\) and
\(\mu-1\). The derivative polynomial is nonzero because its leading
coefficient is \((-1)^k k\). A closed point of the resulting
nonempty fiber gives a reduction of the phase coefficients over the
algebraic closure of \(\mathbb F_p\). The identity \(p\Lambda(f^k)=0\)
would reduce to \(A=0\) there by (1), a contradiction. Here all the
rational denominators of the scaled identity belong to the localized
coefficient ring at \(p\), by the same height bound used in (1).

Finally, fix an exact degree bound and the starting order of a pure
tail. The moments at orders in that tail are polynomials in finitely
many coefficient variables. Their common zero locus is algebraic by
Noetherianity. Add the leading shape and regularity conditions and
the guards on \(r,s\); these give a constructible parameter locus.
Its ratio image is constructible by Chevalley's theorem. A positive
dimensional image on the affine line over \(\mathbb Q\) would contain
its generic point, corresponding to a phase over some characteristic-zero
extension with transcendental ratio. The preceding argument rules this
out. Thus the possible ratios belong to a finite algebraic set for
each fixed tail and degree bound.
