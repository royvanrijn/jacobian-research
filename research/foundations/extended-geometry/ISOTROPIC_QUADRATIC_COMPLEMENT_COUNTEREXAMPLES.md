# Explicit non-power Mathieu counterexamples on isotropic quadratic complements

Let K be any field of characteristic zero. Let q be a nondegenerate isotropic quadratic form on a K-vector space of dimension N>=3. Split a hyperbolic plane and diagonalize its orthogonal complement:

\[
 q=2XY+q_0(Z),\qquad q_0=\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_i Z_i^2,
 \qquad M=N-2\geq1,\quad a_i\in K^*.
\]

Write C=K[X,Y,Z_1,...,Z_M,q^{-1}] and let Lambda:C->K be the unique normalized SO(q) x G_m-invariant linear functional. Its existence and uniqueness are justified in Section 2. Define regular functions

\[
 p=(1+X)^3\frac{Y}{q}-\frac{(X+1)(X+2)}2,
 \qquad H=X^M,
 \qquad F_0=p^M,
 \qquad F_*=p^M(1+p).
\]

The central idea is that the invariant trace on the subalgebra generated by X and Y/q has an elementary weighted polynomial integration model. Its endpoint vanishes to order k+1 for p^k. Raising p to the exact threshold M makes every positive power pure. Multiplication by 1+p retains purity and introduces a prime factor of multiplicity one.

## 1. Exact assertions

A phase f in C is **pure** if Lambda(f^m)=0 for every integer m>=1. Call it **safe** if, for every fixed b in C, Lambda(b f^m)=0 for all sufficiently large m; the cutoff may depend on b. A pure phase which is unsafe is a counterexample to the Mathieu property of ker Lambda.

For k>=0 put

\[
 \mu_k=\frac{k!}{2^k(N/2)_k},\qquad (a)_0=1,
 \qquad (a)_k=a(a+1)\cdots(a+k-1).
\]

**Theorem 1 (all mixed moments and sharp thresholds).** For every integer k>=1 and every integer j>=0,

\[
 \Lambda(X^j p^k)=
 \begin{cases}
 \mu_k\binom{k-M}{k-j},&0\leq j\leq k,\\
 0,&j>k.
 \end{cases}                                                   \tag{1}
\]

The generalized binomial convention is [z^r](1+z)^n=binom(n,r) for every integer n and r>=0; for negative n, binom(n,r)=(-1)^r binom(r-n-1,r). Consequently

\[
 \Lambda(p^k)=(-1)^k\binom{M-1}{k}\mu_k.                         \tag{2}
\]

For every integer a>=1, p^a is pure **if and only if** a>=M. Thus M=N-2 is the least positive pure power of this particular seed. Also M is the least nonnegative monomial marker exponent j for which X^j detects infinitely many powers of p: j<M gives zero for every k>=M, while j>=M gives a nonzero value for every k>=max(M,j). These are statements about this seed and these monomial markers, not a classification of all phases or markers.

**Theorem 2 (two explicit counterexamples).** For every integer m>=1,

\[
 \Lambda(F_0^m)=\Lambda(F_*^m)=0,
 \qquad \Lambda(H F_0^m)=\mu_{Mm}\ne0,
\]

and

\[
 \Lambda(H F_*^m)=\sum_{r=0}^{m}\binom mr\mu_{Mm+r}\ne0.          \tag{3}
\]

The single marker H is independent of m. Every summand in (3) is a strictly positive rational number, so the sum is nonzero in Q and hence in every characteristic-zero K. No ordering of K is assumed.

Moreover F_* is not a proper power even over the algebraic closure: in C tensor_K Kbar it cannot equal u g^e for any unit u, any regular g and any integer e>=2. In particular it is absolutely non-proper-power as a regular function. No radial primitivity, homogeneous balanced degree, or Image-contraction statement is asserted.

Theorems 1--2 cover every isotropic q of dimension at least three, including odd dimensions, and remain valid after every extension of the characteristic-zero base field. They do not settle anisotropic odd-dimensional forms over their ground fields, including the positive-definite real norm-three complement. Dimension two is excluded.

## 2. The normalized invariant trace over the given field

An isotropic nondegenerate quadratic form in characteristic different from two splits a hyperbolic plane: choose a nonzero isotropic vector e, a vector f with nonzero polar pairing, replace f by f-c e to make it isotropic, and scale the pairing. Its orthogonal complement is nondegenerate and has a diagonal basis. This gives the displayed coordinates over K itself.

For N>=3, SO(q) is a smooth connected reductive K-group. After algebraic closure it is the standard special orthogonal group. It acts transitively on each nonzero norm level: an isometry sending one vector to another follows by extending orthogonal bases; if its determinant is -1, compose with a reflection in the nondegenerate orthogonal complement of the target vector. Scalar multiplication changes q by a square, and every nonzero scalar has a square root over Kbar. Hence G=SO(q) x G_m is geometrically transitive on Spec C.

The G-invariants in C are K. Indeed after algebraic closure an invariant regular function is constant on the transitive variety, and scalar descent leaves exactly K. The rational G-module C is locally finite. Over every characteristic-zero field, finite-dimensional rational representations of connected reductive groups are semisimple; locally finite rational modules are sums of simples. This is the precise complete-reducibility input from Milne, Theorem 22.42, Corollary 22.43 and Section 12l. Thus the trivial isotypic component is exactly K*1 and there is one normalized invariant projection Lambda:C->K.

Equivalently C=K*1 directsum Lie(G)C as vector spaces. On a nontrivial simple representation the span of the Lie action is the whole representation: otherwise it is zero, and a connected characteristic-zero group with zero differential image acts trivially. This also proves uniqueness and shows that invariant Lambda kills every infinitesimal derivative. Lie(G)C here is a vector subspace, not an ideal. There is no assumption of compactness, an ordering, an embedding K->R or C, or a K-point on {q=1}. No all-automorphism or general finite-etale trace theorem is used.

## 3. The direct SO Lie recurrence

Put x=X and y=Y/q. The hyperbolic torus in SO(q) sends X to cX, Y to c^{-1}Y and fixes Z_i and q. Thus x and y have weights +1 and -1. Invariance, or its infinitesimal weight a-b, gives

\[
 \Lambda(x^a y^b)=0\quad\hbox{if }a\ne b.                        \tag{4}
\]

This is a split algebraic torus argument over K. For every i, the linear derivation

\[
 D_i=a_i Z_i\partial_Y-X\partial_{Z_i}
\]

annihilates q, so lies in the Lie algebra of SO(q) and extends across q^{-1}. For k>=1,

\[
 D_i\left(\frac{X^{k-1}Y^k Z_i}{q^k}\right)
 =k a_i\frac{(XY)^{k-1}Z_i^2}{q^k}-\left(\frac{XY}{q}\right)^k.
\]

Apply Lambda and sum over i. With mu_k=Lambda((XY/q)^k) and mu_0=1, the identity sum a_i Z_i^2=q-2XY gives

\[
 M\mu_k=k(\mu_{k-1}-2\mu_k),\qquad
 \mu_k=\frac{k}{M+2k}\mu_{k-1}
      =\frac{k!}{2^k(N/2)_k}.                                  \tag{5}
\]

All denominators are nonzero rational integers or half-integers in characteristic zero. In particular mu_k is a nonzero rational number inside K. These identities impose no restrictions on the coefficients a_i beyond nondegeneracy.

## 4. A polynomial integration model

For h(t)=sum h_r t^r in K[t], define the K-linear operation

\[
 L_M(h)=M\int_0^1t^{M-1}h(t)\,dt
       :=\sum_r\frac{M h_r}{M+r}.
\]

The integral is notation for finite polynomial integration, not an analytic integral on K or the law of a chosen sphere coordinate. For g in K[x,y], substitute y=(1-t^2)/(2x), take CT_x in K[t][x,x^{-1}], and apply L_M. Unequal powers x^a y^b vanish. For a=b=k, repeated polynomial integration by parts gives

\[
 L_M\left(\left(\frac{1-t^2}{2}\right)^k\right)
 =\frac{k!}{2^k(M/2+1)_k}=\mu_k.
\]

For example the integral without 2^{-k} has initial value 1 and ratio 2k/(M+2k); this follows by integrating the derivative of t^M(1-t^2)^k and using its zero endpoints for k>=1. Consequently, by (4)--(5), for every polynomial g,

\[
 \Lambda(g(x,y))=L_M\left(\operatorname{CT}_x
 g\left(x,\frac{1-t^2}{2x}\right)\right).                        \tag{6}
\]

The right side is valid on the actual subalgebra generated by X and Y/q because it agrees with Lambda on every polynomial representative. No algebraic independence claim is needed.

## 5. Endpoint cancellation and the full mixed formula

Let U=1+x. Direct simplification of the seed under (6) gives

\[
 p=\frac{U}{2x}(1-t^2 U^2).                                     \tag{7}
\]

For k>=0 define polynomials

\[
 J_k(U)=M\int_0^U w^{M-1}(1-w^2)^k\,dw,
 \qquad A_k(U)=U^{k-M}J_k(U).
\]

Although the second expression displays a possibly negative power, J_k is divisible by U^M. Explicitly

\[
 A_k(U)=M\sum_{r=0}^k(-1)^r\binom kr
                 \frac{U^{k+2r}}{M+2r}\in\mathbb Q[U].          \tag{8}
\]

Termwise substitution w=tU in the finite integral and (7) yield, for j<=k,

\[
 \Lambda(x^j p^k)=2^{-k}[x^{k-j}]A_k(1+x).                     \tag{9}
\]

For j>k the value is zero: in the original polynomial p^k the degree in y is at most k, and multiplication by x^j cannot have equal nonnegative x,y exponents. This also matches the negative coefficient index in (9).

Now

\[
 J_k'(U)=M U^{M-1}(1-U^2)^k
\]

is divisible by (U-1)^k. In characteristic zero this implies

\[
 J_k(U)-J_k(1)\in (U-1)^{k+1}\mathbb Q[U].
\]

At U=1, multiplication by U^{k-M} is multiplication by a unit formal power series even if k<M. Thus

\[
 A_k(1+x)\equiv J_k(1)(1+x)^{k-M}\pmod{x^{k+1}}.               \tag{10}
\]

This use of a formal series concerns only a finite jet of the already polynomial A_k; it does not assert that a general formal series is a polynomial. Since 2^{-k}J_k(1)=mu_k, extracting every coefficient of degree k-j between 0 and k proves (1).

For j=0, binom(k-M,k)=(-1)^k binom(M-1,k). It is nonzero precisely when 0<=k<M. Hence (2), including the zero-moment threshold, follows. For a>=1, a<M makes the first moment of p^a nonzero; a>=M makes every exponent am lie in the zero range. This proves both directions of the pure-power assertion without any converse from purity of a power to purity of its root.

For k>=M the upper binomial argument is nonnegative. If j<M, its lower argument k-j exceeds k-M and the value vanishes. If j>=M and k>=j, it is binom(k-M,j-M), a nonzero positive integer. This proves the exact monomial marker threshold.

## 6. One fixed marker and the non-power phase

Taking k=Mm in (1) gives Lambda(F_0^m)=0 and Lambda(X^M F_0^m)=mu_{Mm} for all m>=1. Every positive power p^a also has infinitely many nonzero values against X^M, once am>=M; for a<M it fails purity already at its first moment.

Expand the actual phase F_*^m=p^{Mm}(1+p)^m. Every exponent Mm+r, 0<=r<=m, is at least M, so (2) kills every pure summand. Formula (1) with j=M gives precisely (3). Each mu_{Mm+r} is a positive rational number. The finite sum with positive integer binomial coefficients is therefore nonzero in Q; injectivity of Q->K proves nonvanishing in K. This is a rational calculation, not positivity in an ordered ground field. These two explicit pure phases show ker Lambda is not Mathieu.

## 7. Absolute non-proper-power

Work over Kbar and write R=Kbar[X,Y,Z_1,...,Z_M]. The following identities hold for arbitrary q_0:

\[
 p=\frac{(X+1)(2Y-(X+2)q_0)}{2q},\qquad
 1+p=\frac{C_1}{2q},\qquad
 C_1=2(3X+1)Y-X(X+3)q_0.                                       \tag{11}
\]

In the UFD Kbar[X,Z_1,...,Z_M], the coefficients of C_1 as a polynomial in Y are coprime. Indeed 3X+1 is irreducible and, at X=-1/3, the other coefficient is (8/9)q_0, a nonzero polynomial. Thus C_1 is primitive and is degree one over the fraction field. Gauss's lemma proves it irreducible in R.

The quadratic q is irreducible over Kbar: a factorization of a homogeneous quadratic into linear forms would give rank at most two, whereas its rank is N>=3. The irreducibles q and C_1 are distinct (their total degrees are two and four). Therefore C_1 remains a prime nonunit in the UFD localization R[q^{-1}]. Its valuation on 1+p is one. Since p and 1+p differ by 1, C_1 cannot divide both; hence its valuation on p is zero. It follows that

\[
 \operatorname{ord}_{C_1}(F_*)=1.
\]

Every unit of this localization has C_1-valuation zero. If F_*=u g^e with u a unit and e>=2, its valuation would be divisible by e, which is impossible. This proves the stronger unit-times-power exclusion stated in Theorem 2. This factor argument proves non-power; the independent moment argument proves purity and failure of the Mathieu property.

## 8. Dimension three, the retained Hopf profile, and scope

For M=1, L_1 is ordinary polynomial integration on [0,1]. All polynomials of t occurring in (6)--(7) are even, so it equals half the integral on [-1,1]. Equations (7)--(10) become the endpoint proof for the signed-height profile r=q=1, gamma=1/2, P(w)=1-w^2 in the retained Hopf Theorem 7.1, equations (7.1)--(7.7). In particular p itself is pure, with marker X and nonzero marked moments at every order. For the direct N=3 endpoint proof, set B_k(U)=integral_0^U (1-w^2)^k dw. Its derivative has a zero of order k at U=1, so B_k(U)-B_k(1) is divisible by (U-1)^(k+1). The model for p^k is 2^{-k}x^{-k}U^{k-1}B_k(U). Its pure constant term is zero, since the endpoint jet is B_k(1)(1+x)^{k-1}; multiplication by x has constant term 2^{-k}B_k(1)=mu_k. Thus the N=3 conclusion follows either by specializing (1) or by this local M=1 calculation, without needing the all-M endpoint theorem.

This is an exact comparison with the old sphere/cover construction. The direct SO recurrence proves the trace on the current complement and the endpoint argument is reproduced here. Neither a prior cover theorem nor the retained real-profile classification is a necessary premise of the all-field result. Over arbitrary K, no real probability law or choice of a square root of a_i is imported.

Over R, every indefinite nondegenerate quadratic form of dimension N>=3 is isotropic, so this theorem applies to all such complements. In odd dimensions it gives the isotropic case; anisotropic odd forms remain outside its hypotheses. The separate even-dimensional quadratic theorem uses another construction and is not a premise here. No claim is made about dimension two, degenerate forms, positive characteristic, arbitrary phases, radial primitiveness, or balanced Image degrees.
