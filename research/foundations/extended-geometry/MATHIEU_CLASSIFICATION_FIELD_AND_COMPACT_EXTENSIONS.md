# Extensions of the Mathieu classifications

Use normalized invariant integration on an affine homogeneous space $X=G/H$ in characteristic zero. A function $P$ is **pure** if $\Lambda(P^m)=0$ at every positive order. It is **safe** if $\Lambda(QP^m)=0$ eventually for every fixed regular function $Q$. Thus the Mathieu–Zhao property says that every pure function is safe. The geometric nullcone consists of the functions whose orbit closure over the algebraic closure contains zero.

The [complex classification](../../../results/mathieu-reductive-homogeneous-spaces/main.tex) supplies the moment identities and the homogeneous-space reductions used below. Mathieu's original compact-group and rational-algebra formulations are in §§1.1–1.7 of [Some conjectures about invariant theory](https://smf.emath.fr/sites/default/files/2018-11/smf_sem-cong_2_263-279__sample.pdf). [Long's Theorems 1.1–1.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.16178v1) classify the compact connected group case and supply the original radial counterexamples.

## Algebraically closed fields

**Theorem.** Let $k$ be an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero, and let $G$ and $H\subseteq G$ be linear algebraic groups with reductive identity components. On $k[G/H]$ the normalized Reynolds kernel is Mathieu–Zhao exactly when $D=[G^\circ,G^\circ]\subseteq H$. If containment fails, a pure $P$ and one fixed $Q$ have nonzero marked moments at infinitely many orders.

When $G$ is connected, equality of the pure set and the geometric nullcone has the same criterion. In general this equality also requires injectivity of
$$
\bigoplus_i X^*(G^\circ/H_i)\ \longrightarrow\ X^*(G^\circ/D),
\qquad (\chi_i)_i\longmapsto\sum_i\chi_i,
$$
where $G^\circ/H_i$ are the components and the map is used when $D\subseteq H$.

### Transferring the radial witness

The case that requires field transfer is the line-and-doublet proposition of the complex classification. Suppose for connected $G$ that an irreducible module $V$ has an $H$-stable line $k\xi$ with character $\chi$, and a homomorphism $S:\mathrm{SL}_2\to G$ has a defining doublet in $V$. Fix a nonzero intertwiner $j:\,k^2\to V$ for that doublet.

All these data, including faithful matrix coordinates, descend to an algebraically closed subfield of $k$ algebraic over a finitely generated extension of $\mathbb Q$. Embed that subfield in $\mathbb C$ by sending a transcendence basis to algebraically independent real numbers. Its image $F$ is the algebraic closure inside $\mathbb C$ of the field generated by these real numbers. It is stable under conjugation, and $R=F\cap\mathbb R$ is real closed. Indeed positive elements have square roots in $R$, and odd-degree polynomials over $R$ have real roots in $F$. Also $F=R(i)$. Reductivity, irreducibility, and the intertwining and character identities here are geometric properties or polynomial identities, so the models have the same properties after these scalar extensions.

Embed $G$ faithfully on a direct sum $E$ containing $V$ as a summand. Write $B$ for a block-diagonal positive definite Hermitian matrix on $E$ and $\dagger$ for the corresponding adjoint, using the standard form on the doublet. Seek $B$ and $s\in G$ with the following properties.

1. The matrix Lie algebra of $G$ is stable under adjoint.
2. The three generators $iH_0,E_0-F_0,i(E_0+F_0)$ of the compact Lie algebra of $\mathrm{SU}_2$ have skew-adjoint images under the conjugate $S_s=\operatorname{Int}(s)\circ S$. Here $H_0,E_0,F_0$ are the usual $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ matrices.
3. The row $\xi^\dagger$ belongs to the $\chi^{-1}$-eigenspace of $H$ in $V^*$.

These are finite polynomial conditions and strict inequalities on real and imaginary parts over $R$. For the first condition introduce coefficients expressing the adjoints of a fixed Lie algebra basis in that basis. For the third use a basis over $F$ of the specified eigenspace. This eigenspace is the kernel of a coaction equation and commutes with base change. Positive definiteness is expressed by the leading principal minors.

A complex solution exists. Take a compact form $L$ of $H$ and a compact form $C$ of $G$ containing $L$. Conjugate the image of $\mathrm{SU}_2$ into $C$ and average the Hermitian forms on the summands over $C$. The action of $L$ on the line is unitary, so the dual row transforms by $\chi^{-1}$ on $L$ and hence on $H$ by Zariski density. The existence and conjugacy of compact forms used here are [Adams–Taïbi, Theorems 3.7–3.8](https://otaibi.perso.math.cnrs.fr/galois.pdf). Quantifier elimination for real closed fields therefore gives a solution with entries in $F$. [Cohen and Mahboubi, §6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1201.3731)

For this solution let $C_B$ be the subgroup of $G(\mathbb C)$ preserving $B$. It is compact. Its skew-adjoint Lie algebra complexifies to the Lie algebra of $G$ by condition 1, so $C_B$ is Zariski dense in connected $G$. Condition 2 puts $S_s(\mathrm{SU}_2)$ in $C_B$. Define $j_s=\rho(s)j$, where $\rho$ acts on $V$, and the regular column and row
$$
z(g)=j_s^\dagger\rho(g)\xi,\qquad
w(g)=\xi^\dagger\rho(g)^{-1}j_s.
$$
On $C_B$, $w(g)=\overline{z(g)}^{\mathsf T}$. The column map $z$ is equivariant for $S_s(\mathrm{SU}_2)$ by unitarity and the intertwining identity for $j_s$. It is nonzero somewhere on $C_B$ because the span of $C_B\xi$ is all of the irreducible $V$ and $j_s^\dagger\ne0$.

On right multiplication by $h\in H$ the column $z$ scales by $\chi(h)$ and the row $w$ by its inverse. Evaluate the two polynomials $P_4,Q_4$ of the complex paper's normalizer witness on the matrix with columns $z(g)$ and $(-w_1(g),w_0(g))^{\mathsf T}$. These polynomials have right diagonal weight zero on the whole matrix space. Therefore this gives a regular pair over $F$ on $G/H$. Normalized integration on $C_B$ is the Reynolds functional after extension to $\mathbb C$. The radial proposition in §5 of the complex paper, applied to the pushforward under $z$, gives purity and strict positivity of every positive marked moment. Injectivity of $F\to\mathbb C$ and base change of the Reynolds projection give the asserted witness over $k$.

Only the finite matrix conditions were transferred between real closed fields. Positivity after that transfer proves the whole infinite sequence of marked inequalities in one step.

### Completing the homogeneous-space classification

For connected $G$, make the central cover used in the complex paper. Its higher-rank root-doublet and diagonal-coupling reductions are algebraic over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero. The doublet condition can also be tested after embedding the algebraic closure of a finitely generated field of definition into $\mathbb C$. The intertwiner space is a kernel of a coaction equation and commutes with scalar extension. An active higher-rank simple factor gives the line through evaluation at $eH$ in the dual of an irreducible submodule of $k[G/H]$. When the remaining rank-one stabilizer projections are all surjective, a nonsingleton diagonal block gives the alternating line. Apply the preceding transfer in these two cases. A proper rank-one projection is covered by the [split rank-one proof](SPLIT_RANK_ONE_HOMOGENEOUS_SPACE_MATHIEU_CLASSIFICATION.md). Equivariant quotient pullback preserves the normalized trace.

The arguments of §7 of the complex paper apply over $k$. A connected-component witness extends by zero to the whole space. If $D\subseteq H$, the components are tori, the residual trace is the average of their constant terms, and finite-trace separation applies in characteristic zero. Apply Duistermaat–van der Kallen to each component. Its support then has a strictly separating functional, which proves safety. The lattice-injectivity condition lets the separate functionals extend to a single geometric cocharacter for each pure tuple. For noninjectivity the relation of component characters in the complex paper gives a pure tuple outside the nullcone. On the negative branch the fixed marked witness excludes geometric instability by the weight-bound argument in [Long's instability lemma](https://github.com/long-mathematics/mathieu-second-conjecture-spherical-pairs/blob/e9edac262b4b64eb68feb0e6e80496cefe4a1dee/mathieu_second_conjecture_spherical_pairs.tex).

For any characteristic-zero field $K$ and such a pair over $K$, the same containment criterion classifies the Mathieu–Zhao property **after every field extension** of $K$. In the positive case apply the algebraically closed result to the algebraic closure of each extension and restrict the trace. In the negative case the geometric witness is defined over a finite extension, since only finitely many coefficients of two regular functions have to be adjoined and all its moment identities commute with base change.

## Split groups over the ground field

**Group case.** For every characteristic-zero field $K$ and every split connected reductive $K$-group $G$, the normalized trace on $K[G]$ has Mathieu–Zhao kernel exactly when $G$ is a torus.

For the negative direction use a split adjoint simple quotient. Start on the simply connected split group of that type over $\mathbb Q$. In the representation of fundamental highest weight corresponding to a simple root, the highest-weight vector and its first lowering give the defining doublet for that root. These data are rational. The compact real Lie algebra for a split Chevalley basis is spanned over $\mathbb R$ by $iH_j$, $E_\alpha-E_{-\alpha}$, $i(E_\alpha+E_{-\alpha})$, with the usual compact conjugation of root vectors. Its compact group is stable under ordinary conjugation in the rational model and contains the root $\mathrm{SU}_2$. The Chevalley compact form is also described in [the rational coadjoint construction](PIECEWISE_DENSITY_AND_COMPACT_COADJOINT_ALL_ORDER_COUNTEREXAMPLES.md).

There is a rational positive definite symmetric Gram matrix on $V$ making this Lie algebra skew-adjoint. Averaging over the compact group gives a positive definite Hermitian solution. Averaging that matrix with its entrywise conjugate makes it real. The resulting real-symmetric skew-adjointness constraints are linear with rational coefficients because each generator is rational or $i$ times rational. Rational points are dense in the real solution space, and positive definiteness is open there. Use such a rational matrix, the rational intertwiner and a nonzero rational vector on the highest-weight line in the preceding radial construction. The central character on that line and its inverse on the dual row cancel, so both regular functions descend to the adjoint group over $\mathbb Q$. Their moments are zero and nonzero, respectively, at every positive order by the compact calculation. They remain so after base change to $K$ and pullback to $G$. The positive direction is scalar Laurent constant-term separation.

**Real closed fields.** For a real closed field $K$ and a homogeneous pair as in the first theorem with $G^\circ$ split, the criterion $[G^\circ,G^\circ]\subseteq H$ holds for the trace on $K[G/H]$ itself.

First take $K=\mathbb R$ and $G$ connected and split. The preceding reduction to a rank-one projection or to a line and a defining doublet works over the field. Split irreducible modules are absolutely irreducible, so the evaluation line and a root doublet can be taken over the field in the higher-rank case. In the subdirect case, a coupling is a graph of automorphisms between split $\mathrm{SL}_2$ factors. An automorphism is conjugation by a matrix in $\mathrm{GL}_2(K)$, so the alternating line after this identification is defined over $K$. The rank-one projection uses the ground-field rank-one proof.

For the line-and-doublet case choose a compact conjugation of $G_\mathbb C$ compatible both with the given real conjugation and with a compact conjugation of $H_\mathbb C$. This is [Adams–Taïbi, Theorem 3.13 and Lemma 3.14](https://otaibi.perso.math.cnrs.fr/galois.pdf), including disconnected $H$. The compact group is stable under ordinary conjugation, so the Hermitian matrices can be taken real symmetric by averaging each with its conjugate. For the fixed root subgroup use the compact conjugation of the split Chevalley model, whose compact form contains its $\mathrm{SU}_2$. The uniqueness statement in Theorem 3.13 conjugates this compact form to the desired one by $s\in G(\mathbb R)^\circ$. The same radial proof now uses real matrices and gives real witnesses.

Here is the transfer to arbitrary real closed $K$. Over a finitely generated subfield $E\subset K$ containing the pair, its split-model isomorphism and the split representation data, the compact-matrix conditions are a finite existential formula with $B$ real symmetric and $s$ real. Every embedding $E\to\mathbb R$ admits a solution by the preceding argument. Any finite list of strict sign conditions on elements of $E$ that holds in $K$ can be preserved by such an embedding. To see this, write $E=\mathbb Q(t_1,\ldots,t_d)[\alpha]$ with $\alpha$ separable, and express the signs, nonzero denominators and nonvanishing derivative of the minimal polynomial as a finite real formula over $\mathbb Q$. It has a solution over $\mathbb R$ by Tarski. By the real implicit function theorem the solution branch persists on an open set of parameter values. Pick algebraically independent real parameter values in that set to obtain a field embedding. For $d=0$ use the real root from the same formula.

Quantifier elimination now gives the compact-matrix solution over $K$. Otherwise finitely many signs would force failure over $\mathbb R$ along an embedding preserving those signs. To verify the moments, adjoin the selected matrix entries to the finitely generated field and embed it in $\mathbb R$ preserving the positivity of the leading principal minors. This single embedding verifies every moment of the pair via the radial proof and injectivity. Thus the witness is defined over $K$. For disconnected $G$ use the open-and-closed $K$-subspace $G^\circ H/H$ for the negative branch and extend the pair by zero. For the positive branch the criterion after algebraic closure already implies the ground-field property.

### A split homogeneous example over every characteristic-zero field

For $n\ge3$, let $G=\mathrm{SL}_n$ and let $H=S(\mathrm{GL}_{n-1}\times\mathrm{GL}_1)$ be block diagonal. Over $\mathbb Q$ take $z$ to be the first two entries of $g e_n$ and $w$ the first two entries of $e_n^{\mathsf T}g^{-1}$. The last coordinate character of $H$ and its inverse cancel in the radial polynomials. On $\mathrm{SU}(n)$, $w=\overline z^{\mathsf T}$, and the squared norm of $z$ is the sum of the first two squared moduli of a uniformly distributed unit vector in $\mathbb C^n$. Those moduli have the Dirichlet law with parameters all one. This follows by normalizing independent standard complex Gaussians, whose squared moduli are independent exponentials. Hence $r=\|z\|^2$ has the beta law with parameters $(2,n-2)$. For the same fixed pair as in the radial construction,
$$
 \Lambda(P^m)=0,\qquad
 \Lambda(QP^m)=\beta_m\frac{(2)_{6m-2}}{(n)_{6m-2}},
 \qquad
 \beta_m=\frac{2m}{3m+1}\frac{(2m)!(4m)!}{(6m)!}.
$$
Here $(a)_j=a(a+1)\cdots(a+j-1)$. The formulas and the fixed pair extend from $\mathbb Q$ to every characteristic-zero field. For $\mathrm{SL}_3/S(\mathrm{GL}_2\times\mathrm{GL}_1)$ the first marked moment is $1/90$.

## The universal algebra formulation of Mathieu I

Let $K$ be a characteristic-zero field and $G$ a linear algebraic $K$-group with reductive identity component. The following are equivalent.

- $G^\circ$ is a torus.
- For every commutative rational $G$-algebra $A$, the canonical Reynolds projection $R_G:A\to A^G$ has a Mathieu–Zhao kernel.
- For every such $A$ and every $f\in A$ with $R_G(f^m)=0$ for every $m\ge1$, each fixed geometric isotypic component of $f^m$ vanishes eventually.

A geometric isotypic component is taken after scalar extension to an algebraic closure. This is the isotypic condition in Mathieu's Corollary 1.7. To prove the positive direction, pass to the algebraic closure and take the finitely generated $G$-stable subalgebra $B$ generated by a finite-dimensional submodule containing $f$. Put $T=G^\circ$, choose component representatives $g_1=1,g_2,\ldots,g_r$, and let $L_i$ be the Laurent polynomial that records the $T$-weights of $g_i f$ with their coefficients in $B$. Tori act by a grading, so taking the constant coefficient of $L_i^m$ gives $R_T((g_i f)^m)$. Averaging over the components gives
$$
 \sum_{i=1}^r \operatorname{CT}(L_i^m)=0\qquad(m\ge1).
$$
Take a primary decomposition $0=\bigcap_j\mathfrak q_j$ of $B$. For each $j$, put $\mathfrak p_j=\sqrt{\mathfrak q_j}$ and choose $e_j$ with $\mathfrak p_j^{e_j}\subseteq\mathfrak q_j$. In the fraction field of $B/\mathfrak p_j$, the finite-trace lemma of the complex paper applies to the Laurent polynomials $L_i$ and gives zero pure constant terms for each separately.

Write $L_1=U+V$ by putting the coefficients in $\mathfrak p_j$ in $V$. If $U$ is nonzero, the theorem of Duistermaat–van der Kallen gives a functional strictly positive on its support. The theorem applies in characteristic zero by embedding the finitely generated field of the coefficients into $\mathbb C$. For a fixed weight, the terms of $(U+V)^m$ with fewer than $e_j$ factors of $V$ eventually cannot reach that weight. Terms with at least $e_j$ such factors have coefficients in $\mathfrak q_j$. If $U=0$, all coefficients are in $\mathfrak q_j$ for $m\ge e_j$. Since the number of primary components is finite, each fixed weight coefficient of $f^m$ is eventually in their intersection, hence zero. A simple $G$-module has finitely many $T$-weights. Vanishing of these weight components implies vanishing of its whole isotypic projection. Likewise a fixed multiplier has finitely many weights, so eventually the product with $f^m$ has zero weight-zero component and therefore zero Reynolds projection.

If $G^\circ$ is non-toral the homogeneous theorem gives a witness on $\overline K[G_{\overline K}]$. The pair lives on $G_E$ over some finite extension $E/K$. Regard $E[G_E]$ as a rational $G$-algebra over $K$. Its invariants are $E$, and the $G$-projection is the normalized trace with values in $E$. The pair contradicts the universal kernel condition. It also contradicts the universal isotypic condition, since trace of a product pairs only dual types and a fixed multiplier has finitely many types. This argument permits disconnected $G$ and arbitrary commutative rational $G$-algebras, including nonreduced ones.

## Compact groups and compact homogeneous spaces

Let $C$ be any compact Hausdorff group, $L$ a closed subgroup, and use the complex-valued $C$-finite functions on $C/L$. Then the normalized invariant integral has Mathieu–Zhao kernel exactly when
$$
 \overline{[C^\circ,C^\circ]}\ \subseteq\ L.
$$
In particular the group case holds exactly when $C^\circ$ is abelian.

For a compact Lie quotient $J$ of $C$, write $L_J$ for the image of $L$. The compact Lie pair $J/L_J$ has the complexification given by the Zariski closures of a faithful unitary model of $J$ and its closed subgroup $L_J$. These closures are reductive on identity components. Representative functions on $J$ are the restrictions of regular functions on the closure. One can see this by using the matrix-entry algebra, which contains conjugate entries since the model is unitary. It is dense by Stone–Weierstrass, so projection onto each finite-dimensional Peter–Weyl isotype is onto. Right invariance is equivalent to invariance under the closure of $L_J$. The intersection of that closure with $J$ is $L_J$, since averaging matrix-entry polynomials over $L_J$ separates distinct right cosets. The identity component $J^\circ$ is Zariski dense in the identity component of its complexification. Consequently the preprint applies to $J/L_J$, with positive criterion $[J^\circ,J^\circ]\subseteq L_J$.

Any two representative functions on $C/L$ factor together through a compact Lie quotient $J/L_J$ by taking the direct sum of their finite-dimensional representations. The image of $C^\circ$ in $J$ is $J^\circ$. Indeed the quotient by the image is a quotient of the profinite group $C/C^\circ$ and is totally disconnected. Haar measure pushes forward to the normalized invariant measure on the quotient. Thus containment in $L$ implies safety by the Lie quotient criterion. Conversely if an element of $[C^\circ,C^\circ]$ lies outside $L$, a finite-dimensional representation separates that element from the compact set $L$. To choose it, cover $L$ by finitely many point-separating Peter–Weyl neighborhoods and take the direct sum. The resulting compact Lie quotient has a bad pair, which pulls back with its marked moments intact. [Peter–Weyl and compact Lie quotients](https://www.sas.rochester.edu/mth/sites/doug-ravenel/otherpapers/fausk.pdf)

For the corresponding geometric nullcone condition on compact homogeneous spaces, use the Zariski closure of the $C$-orbit of a representative function in its finite-dimensional complex orbit span. Equality with the pure set holds exactly when every compact Lie quotient pair $J/L_J$ satisfies the lattice criterion of the complex classification. Both the pure condition and that orbit closure are computed in a common Lie quotient for each individual function. When $C$ is connected every Lie quotient is connected, so this criterion is again $\overline{[C,C]}\subseteq L$.

For $C=\mathrm O(2)$ and $L=\{1\}$ the Mathieu–Zhao property holds. In coordinates $z$ on the rotation circle and on its translate by a reflection, the representative function $(z,z^{-1})$ is pure and safe. Its two nonzero components have opposite weights for the same identity torus. Reflection interchanges the two spanning functions. The product of the coordinate functions on their span is therefore an invariant equal to one at this function, so its orbit closure excludes zero.

## Ground-field witnesses on Weil restrictions

Let $E/K$ be finite separable in characteristic zero and let $Y=G/H$ be an affine reductive homogeneous space over $E$. If $(P,Q)$ is a fixed witness on $Y$, the Weil restriction $\operatorname{Res}_{E/K}Y$ has a fixed witness over $K$ with exactly the same surviving marked orders. The two functions are the norms of the pullbacks of $P,Q$ to the base extension of the restriction to $E$. After scalar extension to $\overline K$, the restriction is the product of $Y^\sigma$ over all $K$-embeddings $\sigma:E\hookrightarrow\overline K$, and each norm is the product of the corresponding conjugate functions. This product descends to $K$ by Galois invariance. The restriction is homogeneous under $\operatorname{Res}_{E/K}G$ with stabilizer $\operatorname{Res}_{E/K}H$, as can be checked on this product after faithfully flat extension. Normalized traces multiply on products, hence
$$
 \Lambda_{\operatorname{Res}_{E/K}Y}(Q_{\rm N}P_{\rm N}^{m})
 =N_{E/K}\bigl(\Lambda_Y(QP^m)\bigr).
$$
The same formula for $Q=1$ proves purity. For example, a non-toral split connected group over $E$ gives a ground-field counterexample on its restriction of scalars over $K$.

## The adjoint radial pair

Write a traceless matrix as
$$
Z=\begin{pmatrix}a/2&v\\u&-a/2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
r(Z)=a^2+4uv.
$$
On the split three-dimensional adjoint representation, put
$$
P_{\rm ad}=v^2(1+au-u^3v)^4,\qquad Q_{\rm ad}=u^2.
$$
For the Reynolds projection $R_S$ of $S=\mathrm{SL}_2$ acting by conjugation, one has the polynomial identities
$$
 R_S(P_{\rm ad}^{m})=0,\qquad
 R_S(Q_{\rm ad}P_{\rm ad}^{m})=\beta_m r^{3m-1}\quad (m\ge1).
$$
Indeed, for a column $w=(x,y)^{\mathsf T}$ over $\mathbb C$, set $Z=ww^*-\|w\|^2 I/2$. Then $a=|x|^2-|y|^2$, $u=\bar x y$, $v=x\bar y$, and $r(Z)=\|w\|^4$. In the normalizer pair of the complex paper the two factors become $1+|x|^2u$ and $1-|y|^2u$. Their product is $1+au-u^3v$ since $uv=|x|^2|y|^2$. This gives exactly $P_{\rm ad}$ and $Q_{\rm ad}$. The map $w\mapsto Z$ is $\mathrm{SU}_2$-equivariant and reaches every trace-zero Hermitian matrix, by diagonalizing it and choosing $w$ on its nonnegative eigenline. Angular averaging on each sphere therefore gives the two identities on this real form and hence on the entire adjoint space. The coefficients and normalized projections commute with base change from $\mathbb Q$. The action and the proof also work through $\mathrm{PGL}_2$. [Complex classification, §5](https://hexagonmath.org/pdf/2611.00556v1)

## Isotropic derived groups over arbitrary characteristic-zero fields

**Theorem.** Let $G$ be connected reductive over any characteristic-zero field $K$. If $G$ admits a nontrivial $K$-homomorphism from split $\mathrm{SL}_2$, then the trace on $K[G]$ has a fixed witness with nonzero marked moments at every positive order. In particular this applies if the derived group has positive $K$-split rank.

Let $\mathfrak s=dS(\mathfrak{sl}_2)$ for such a homomorphism $S$, let $\mathfrak d=\operatorname{Lie}([G,G])$, and let $p:\mathfrak d\to\mathfrak s$ be the Killing-orthogonal projection. The restriction of the Killing form is nondegenerate. Over a splitting field it is a scalar multiple of the Killing form of $\mathfrak{sl}_2$, and its evaluation on $h=dS(\operatorname{diag}(1,-1))$ is a positive integer by the sum of squared integral weights in $\mathfrak d$. Consequently $p$ is defined over $K$ and equivariant for $S$. Set
$$
 Z(g)=(dS)^{-1}p(\operatorname{Ad}(g)h).
$$
Pull back $P_{\rm ad},Q_{\rm ad}$ along this regular map. To check the marked values, reduce the finitely many model coefficients to a subfield embedded in $\mathbb C$. Choose a compact form $C$ of $G_\mathbb C$ containing $S(\mathrm{SU}_2)$. Its conjugation preserves the Killing projection. Since $h$ is $i$ times a vector in the compact Lie algebra of $S$, for every $g\in C$ the matrix $Z(g)$ is Hermitian. On this real form $r$ is nonnegative, and $Z(1)=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$, so the trace of $r(Z(g))^{3m-1}$ is strictly positive for every $m$. The adjoint identities and normalized trace base change prove the assertions over $K$. A nontrivial split torus in the derived group gives a nonidentity rational unipotent in a parabolic radical. Rational Jacobson–Morozov produces the required split rank-one homomorphism, which may have central kernel. [Compact forms](https://otaibi.perso.math.cnrs.fr/galois.pdf) · [Rational Jacobson–Morozov, §5.8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.07732)

The same witness works on every affine $G/H$ over $K$ for which $H$ fixes $h$ under the adjoint action. In that case the map $Z$ itself descends and normalized integration equals restriction. For example this applies to the centralizer of the split rank-one cocharacter and its reductive subgroups.

## A split central character and a rank-one subgroup

**Theorem.** Over any characteristic-zero field $K$, let $G$ be connected reductive with a nontrivial $K$-character $\chi:G\to\mathbb G_m$. Suppose $[G,G]$ contains a $K$-subgroup $S$ whose geometric Lie algebra has type $A_1$. Then $K[G]$ has a fixed witness with nonzero marked moments at every positive order. The same conclusion holds for affine $G/H$ if $\chi|_H=1$ and $H$ fixes the chosen nonisotropic vector below.

On $\mathfrak s=\operatorname{Lie}(S)$ let $q$ be the negative of the Killing form of $\mathfrak d$ restricted to $\mathfrak s$, and write $\langle\,,\,\rangle_q$ for its polarization with $\langle x,x\rangle_q=q(x)$. Choose $\xi\in\mathfrak s(K)$ with $q(\xi)\ne0$, and use the Killing projection $p:\mathfrak d\to\mathfrak s$ as above. Define
$$
 z(g)=p(\operatorname{Ad}(g)\xi),\quad
 A(g)=q(z(g)),\quad B(g)=\frac{\langle \xi,z(g)\rangle_q^2}{q(\xi)},\quad t(g)=\chi(g).
$$
The pair is
$$
 P=t^{-1}(1+t)\bigl\{A-(1+t)^2 B\bigr\}^{2},\qquad Q=t .
$$
These expressions descend to $G/H$ under the stated stabilizer conditions.

For the proof embed a field of definition in $\mathbb C$. The adjoint action of the complex rank-one subgroup is transitive on each nonzero quadric level. By choosing its compact form by conjugation, write $\xi=c\xi_0$ with $\xi_0$ in the compact Lie algebra and $c\in\mathbb C^\times$. Choose a compatible compact form of the derived group. It suffices to compute with $\xi_0$, since replacing it by $c\xi_0$ scales the phase by $c^4$ and leaves the marker fixed. The associated $z$ under compact derived Haar measure lies in the compact three-dimensional real form of $\mathfrak s$. Its law is rotation-invariant by $S$-equivariance. Conditional on each nonzero radius, $B/A$ is the square of a coordinate of a uniform point of the unit sphere. That coordinate is uniform on $[-1,1]$.

The central isogeny $Z(G)^\circ\times [G,G]\to G$ allows separate normalized averaging. The variable $z$ is unchanged by central translation, and $t$ pulls back to a nontrivial central character. Its normalized Laurent average therefore extracts the constant coefficient in $t$. On each nonzero angular sphere, divide the phase by $A^2$. The average of its $m$th power is
$$
 t^{-m}(1+t)^{m-1}F_m(1+t),\qquad
 F_m(s)=\int_0^s(1-w^2)^{2m}\,dw.
$$
This identity follows by substituting $(1+t)w$ into the angular integral on $[0,1]$. Since $F_m(1+t)-F_m(1)$ is divisible by $t^{2m+1}$, the constant term above is zero. After multiplication by $t$ it is $F_m(1)>0$. Restoring the radius gives zero pure moments and the positive marked values $F_m(1)\int A^{2m}$ for $\xi_0$. At the radius zero the integrands vanish, and the radius is nonzero on an open neighborhood of the identity. This proves nonvanishing after the rescaling by $c$ and hence over $K$. The angular circuit is the ternary case of the [quadratic-complement normalizer](QUADRATIC_COMPLEMENT_UNIFORM_NORMALIZER_COUNTEREXAMPLES.md).

## Complete connected group classification over real closed fields

**Theorem.** Let $K$ be real closed and $G$ connected reductive. The normalized trace on $K[G]$ has Mathieu–Zhao kernel exactly when $G$ is a torus or $G$ is $K$-anisotropic. Equivalently, in the noncommutative case the kernel is Mathieu–Zhao precisely when the Killing form of the derived Lie algebra is negative definite and $G$ has no nontrivial $K$-character.

Here are the two negative cases and the positive case.

1. If the derived Killing form is not negative definite, its nondegeneracy gives a vector of positive Killing square. The real Lie algebra analogue contains a split $\mathfrak{sl}_2$. Indeed, in a real Cartan decomposition take a nonzero vector $X$ in the noncompact summand. Its adjoint endomorphism is self-adjoint for the positive definite Cartan form. It has a nonzero real eigenvalue, and an eigenvector for that value is adjoint-nilpotent because it shifts the finitely many eigenspaces by that nonzero value. Apply Jacobson–Morozov. The existence of the triple descends from $\mathbb R$ to a real closed $K$ by quantifier elimination and the finite-sign embedding argument above, keeping one positive Killing square. Integrate the triple to the split rank-one homomorphism. The adjoint-pair theorem applies.
2. If the derived Killing form is negative definite and the derived group is nontrivial, choose a maximal $K$-torus in it. It is anisotropic because a nontrivial split cocharacter in a semisimple group would give a positive Killing square by the sum of squared weights. Over a real closed field, Galois conjugation thus acts by $-1$ on its character lattice. The root subgroups for any opposite pair are interchanged by conjugation, so the rank-one subgroup they generate is defined over $K$. If $G$ has a nontrivial $K$-character, apply the central-character theorem.
3. If the derived Killing form is negative definite and there is no nontrivial $K$-character, the connected center is anisotropic. Over a real closed field every such torus is a product of norm-one tori for $K(i)/K$, by its character lattice with conjugation $-1$. For a nonzero $f$ over $K$, take a finitely generated field of definition including $f$ and that torus isomorphism. Embed it into $\mathbb R$ preserving the signs that give negative definiteness. On the real model the connected center and the connected real semisimple group are compact, so the real identity component of $G$ is compact and Zariski dense. The trace of $f^2$ is strictly positive by normalized Haar integration. Injectivity gives a nonzero square trace over $K$, so only zero is pure. This square-trace argument also works for every affine $G/H$ with such a connected compact-type $G$, by pullback from the group. The torus branch follows from the Laurent theorem.

The equivalence with anisotropy follows because the split triple in case 1 gives a split torus, and a split torus in the derived group gives a positive Killing square. In the center a nontrivial split subtorus is equivalent to a nontrivial character, using the central isogeny and clearing a finite character index. The criterion applies to connected reductive quotients by normal stabilizers as well. For a real example with the second mechanism, $\mathrm{SL}_1(\mathbb H)\times\mathbb R^\times$ fails the Mathieu–Zhao property despite the square-positive trace on the norm-one factor.

For a general formally real $K$, a real closure at even one ordering in which a connected group is anisotropic proves square-trace anisotropy for every homogeneous quotient over $K$. Conversely the two fixed-witness theorems above apply over $K$ itself. The following sections extend the noncompact branch to arbitrary reductive stabilizers.

## Arbitrary stabilizers with an active split rank-one action

**Theorem.** Let $G$ be connected reductive over a real closed field $K$, and let $H$ be a closed subgroup with reductive identity component. If a $K$-homomorphism $S=\mathrm{SL}_2\to G$ acts nontrivially on $X=G/H$, then there are $P,Q\in K[X]$ such that $\Lambda_X(P^m)=0$ at every positive order and $\Lambda_X(QP^m)\ne0$ at infinitely many orders. No adjoint-fixed vector for $H$ is required.

In particular, over a real closed field, an active noncompact almost simple factor of the derived group forces failure for every reductive stabilizer. Its relative split rank is positive, so rational Jacobson–Morozov gives a split rank-one action. The action is nontrivial because the kernel on an almost simple factor is central or the whole factor. The field-extension argument in the group classification above produces the rank-one subgroup directly from a positive Killing square.

### Closed orbits and the stabilizer map

First work over $\mathbb C$, with a compact form $C$ of $G$ for which the compact subgroup $S_c=\mathrm{SU}_2$ maps into $C$ and a compact form of $H$ is contained in $C$. For a fixed homomorphism $S$ one may conjugate $H$ into a position compatible with $C$, then use the $G$-equivariant isomorphism of the two quotients. Denote the induced compact conjugation of the quotient by $\tau_X$, and its compact orbit by $C\cdot eH$ in the compatible model. This orbit is Zariski dense and is pointwise fixed by $\tau_X$.

Every point on this compact orbit has closed $S_{\mathbb C}$-orbit. Indeed, embed the affine quotient into a finite-dimensional $S_\mathbb C$-module with compatible real form by choosing an $S_\mathbb C$-stable space of algebra generators closed under $\tau_X$. Give it a compact invariant Hermitian form real on that real form. At a real vector the derivatives of the norm squared in the $i\operatorname{Lie}(S_c)$ directions vanish, since the compact Lie algebra acts on the real form by skew-symmetric matrices. Kempf–Ness gives closedness. [Kempf–Ness](https://doi.org/10.1007/BFb0066647)

The categorical quotient $X/\!/S$ has an open principal Luna stratum. Closed orbits over it have conjugate stabilizers. A point of the compact orbit can be chosen both above this stratum and in the open set of maximal orbit dimension, by Zariski density. Thus the principal closed orbits have maximal dimension. A nonclosed orbit in the same fiber would have strictly larger dimension than its closed orbit, which is impossible. Hence over the principal stratum there is an invariant dense open $U\subseteq X$ consisting of closed orbits with conjugate stabilizers. The principal stratum and $U$ descend to any field of definition by uniqueness and base change of the invariant algebra. These arguments over $\mathbb C$ apply to models in every algebraically closed characteristic-zero field by working over algebraic closures of finitely generated subfields. [Luna, Slices étales](https://numdam.org/articles/10.24033/msmf.110/)

For a characteristic-zero field $K$, a connected reductive group has Zariski dense $K$-points. One argument is to choose finitely many regular semisimple vectors spanning its Lie algebra, and take the associated maximal $K$-tori. The differential of their multiplication map is surjective at the identity. Each such torus has dense $K$-points since it is dominated by restriction of scalars of a split torus via the norm from a finite splitting field. The product map therefore has dense image on $K$-points. In particular we may choose $x_0\in U(K)$ in the image of $G(K)\cdot eH$ and put $L=S_{x_0}$. Since its geometric orbit is closed and affine, $L$ is reductive. It is proper because the action is nontrivial.

If $L$ is noncentral, its normalizer $N=N_S(L)$ is a proper reductive subgroup of $S$. In rank one this follows directly from the subgroup list. A positive-dimensional proper reductive subgroup has a torus as its identity component. For a noncentral finite subgroup the normalizer preserves its torus centralizer in the cyclic case and is finite otherwise. On $U$ there is an $S$-equivariant rational map
$$
 \phi:U\dashrightarrow S/N,\qquad x\longmapsto sN
       \quad\text{when }S_x=sLs^{-1}.
$$
Indeed, the image of $S\times U^L\to U\times S/N$, $(s,y)\mapsto(sy,sN)$, is constructible and has exactly one geometric point over every point of $U$. Here inclusion $L\subseteq S_y$ implies equality because their stabilizers are conjugate, with the same dimension and number of components. In characteristic zero the closure of the graph is birational to $U$. To see this, any dominating component has constructible image containing an open of $U$, and generically finite projection of degree one since the constructible graph has a single geometric point over each general point. There can be only one dominating component by the same uniqueness. This proves the stated rational map over $K$.

### Clearing denominators without losing the marked moments

Take the fixed witness $P_0,Q_0$ on the split rank-one quotient $S/N$ from the [split rank-one theorem](SPLIT_RANK_ONE_HOMOGENEOUS_SPACE_MATHIEU_CLASSIFICATION.md). Pull back the two functions rationally by $\phi$. Their $S$-translates span a finite-dimensional submodule of $K(X)$ by equivariance. The common denominator ideal $I$ of this submodule in $K[X]$ is a nonzero $S$-stable ideal.

For the real closed field proof, arrange the compact conjugation to commute with real conjugation on $G_{K(i)}$ and preserve $H$. Also arrange that it restricts to the standard compact conjugation on the split $S$, after conjugating the homomorphism by a point of $G(K)$. Make this change before forming $U,\phi,I$, or transport them by the same left translation. Over $\mathbb R$ this follows by extending the Cartan involutions for $H$ and for split $S$ separately and conjugating the two Cartan involutions by a real point of $G^\circ$. The extension for $H$ includes disconnected stabilizers. [Adams–Taïbi, Theorem 3.13 and Lemma 3.14](https://otaibi.perso.math.cnrs.fr/galois.pdf)

Here are finite conditions for this argument over a general real closed field. In a faithful real matrix representation seek a real symmetric positive definite matrix $B$ such that $g\mapsto B^{-1}(g^{\mathsf T})^{-1}B$ preserves $G$ and $H$. Require it to restrict on the conjugated image of $S$ to the transpose-inverse involution of $\mathrm{SL}_2$. This is a finite first-order condition over a field of definition, with real and imaginary entries used for the complex group equations, and the conjugating element a real variable. The inclusions and the restriction are expressed using the finite defining equations of $G,H,S$. When the conjugation is given, averaging real matrix forms on the conjugation-stable compact group produces such a $B$. Thus the conditions hold over $\mathbb R$ for every embedding of the model into $\mathbb R$. Quantifier elimination and the finite-sign embedding argument earlier in this note give the desired data over $K$. The Cartan involution $\theta$ on $X$ is then defined over $K$, and $\tau_X=\theta\circ\sigma$ where $\sigma$ is real conjugation. All positivity checks below can be made after embedding a finitely generated field of the selected data into $\mathbb R$, preserving the positive principal minors of $B$.

Take $0\ne a\in I$ and set $d=R_S(a\cdot\theta^*a)\in I^S$. On the compact orbit, $\theta^*a=\bar a$. Consequently $d$ is nonnegative there and positive at some point, by density of the compact orbit and continuity of the angular average. Thus $\Lambda_X(d^j)\ne0$ for every $j\ge1$. Define the regular pair
$$
 P=d(P_0\circ\phi),\qquad Q=d(Q_0\circ\phi).
$$
On the invariant open of definition, rank-one averaging and equivariance give
$$
 R_S(P^m)=0,\qquad
 R_S(QP^m)=d^{m+1}\Lambda_{S/N}(Q_0P_0^m).
$$
Both identities extend to the whole quotient since both sides are regular. Finally $\Lambda_X\circ R_S=\Lambda_X$. This proves the required moments with the same infinite set of surviving marked orders as the rank-one witness.

### Central stabilizers

If the principal $L$ is central, its closed orbit has adjoint matrix coefficients. The restriction onto the coordinate ring of a closed orbit is surjective, and rational $S$-modules are semisimple in characteristic zero. It follows that $K[X]$ contains the adjoint module, by base change of the intertwiner space. Choose a nonzero $K$-defined equivariant map $Z:X\to\mathfrak{sl}_2$.

With the compatible involutions above, $Z\mapsto \tau_{\rm h}\circ Z\circ\tau_X$, where $\tau_{\rm h}(M)=\bar M^{\mathsf T}$, is a $K$-linear involution of the space of real equivariant maps. It preserves that space because both compact conjugations commute with real conjugation and $\tau_{\rm h}$ intertwines the compact group conjugation. Choose a nonzero map in either eigenspace. Its values on the compact orbit are Hermitian in the positive eigenspace and skew-Hermitian in the negative eigenspace. In the latter case use $iZ$ for the moment calculation. Both adjoint witness polynomials have only even total degrees, so their compositions with $iZ$ are regular over $K$. The compact angular identity and the nonzero radius on an open part of the compact orbit give nonzero marked moments at every positive order. This finishes the split rank-one theorem.

### A quotient where the adjoint covariant is absent

Take $G=\mathrm{SL}_3$ and $H=\mathrm{SO}(J)$ with $J=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,-1)$. The affine quotient is the symmetric matrices $M=gJg^{\mathsf T}$ with $\det M=\det J$. Use the split rank-one group $\mathrm{SO}(J)$ acting on the left. The matrix $B=MJ^{-1}$ transforms by conjugation. At a generic point $B$ has distinct eigenvalues and is self-adjoint for $J^{-1}$. Its three eigenlines are orthogonal and nonisotropic. Its stabilizer in $\mathrm{SO}(J)$ is the Klein four group of sign changes of the eigenlines. It fixes no vector in the adjoint module, so this action has no nonzero adjoint covariant on the quotient. The inverse image of the stabilizer in $\mathrm{SL}_2$ is $Q_8$ and its normalizer is $2O$. Sending $B$ to its unordered orthogonal eigenlines is the rational stabilizer map in this case.

This example also works over every characteristic-zero field. On the locus of distinct eigenvalues the map is regular. After the finite étale cover ordering the roots, the eigenprojectors are given by Lagrange interpolation in $B$, and the unordered triple descends. Thus powers of the discriminant $\Delta(B)$ clear the denominators of the pullback of the octahedral witness. On the compact orbit of $\mathrm{SU}(3)$, $B=gJg^{\mathsf T}J^{-1}$ is unitary of determinant one. The discriminant is real there: inverting all eigenvalues preserves the discriminant when their product is one, and complex conjugation inverts the eigenvalues of a unitary matrix. A sufficiently large even power of $\Delta$ is therefore a valid nonnegative denominator, strictly positive at some point. Everything is defined over $\mathbb Q$, so the marked identities persist over every characteristic-zero extension.

## The same obstruction over uncountable fields

Let $K$ be any uncountable characteristic-zero field. The active split rank-one theorem holds over $K$ without an ordering hypothesis. In the noncentral case construct the rational stabilizer map and the denominator ideal over $K$ as above. Work over the algebraic closure of a finitely generated field of definition embedded in $\mathbb C$. Choose the complex compact form compatible with the action and the quotient as above. There exists a complex denominator $d_{\mathbb C}\in I_{\mathbb C}^S$ with all its positive pure moments nonzero, by the compact-square construction. Since invariant projection and the denominator ideal commute with scalar extension, $d_{\mathbb C}$ is a linear combination of finitely many invariant denominators in the field of definition. For the latter assertion the common denominator ideal is the kernel of the map $K[X]\to (K(X)/K[X])^r$ given by multiplication by a finite basis of rational functions. Flat base change, or clearing the chosen finite common denominator first, gives the kernel identity.

On the finite-dimensional parameter space spanned by these denominators, each polynomial $d\mapsto\Lambda_X(d^j)$ is nonzero, since $d_{\mathbb C}$ has nonzero moments. Over an uncountable field there is a point outside the union of these countably many zero sets. This follows by induction on the dimension, choosing the first coordinates so each univariate specialized polynomial remains nonzero, then avoiding countably many roots in the last coordinate. Use the resulting $K$-denominator in the same clearing argument. In the central case take a complex equivariant map that is Hermitian on the compact orbit and express it in a finite span of maps over the field of definition. The same countable avoidance applied to the polynomials $Z\mapsto\Lambda_X(r(Z)^{3m-1})$ gives one $K$-map with all the required marked values nonzero. This argument uses uncountability only for the simultaneous nonvanishing specialization.

## The remaining compact-derived case over real closed fields

Suppose $G$ is connected and $K$ is real closed. Once all active noncompact factors have been handled, quotient out the inactive noncompact factors, which are normal and act trivially. The resulting derived group is of compact type. Up to a central isogeny one may write $G=T_s\times G_c$, where $T_s$ is split central and $G_c$ is of compact type. Taking the preimage of $H$ preserves the homogeneous space. The projection of the connected stabilizer to $T_s$ is a subtorus that is itself contained in the stabilizer: the connected center of the stabilizer maps onto that projection, its anisotropic part has no split characters, and its split part maps trivially to $G_c$. Remove this subtorus. With the resulting notation $T$, the stabilizer has finite projection $\Gamma$ to $T$, and it has projection $L'$ to $G_c$. The product map gives an equivariant surjection
$$
 (T\times G_c)/H\ \longrightarrow\ (T/\Gamma)\times(G_c/L').
$$
The kernel of projection to $\Gamma$ in $H$ identifies with $H\cap G_c$, whose image has finite index in $L'$. Consequently the compact derived group acts trivially on the product target exactly when it acts trivially on the original space. If $T$ is trivial, compact square positivity applies, and if the derived action is trivial, scalar Laurent separation applies.

When both factors act nontrivially, use a nontrivial character of $T/\Gamma$ and choose an active compact rank-one subgroup of $G_c$. Such a subgroup exists by taking an opposite root pair in an active compact almost simple factor. Apply the same principal-orbit construction to $G_c/L'$. If the principal rank-one stabilizer is central, the adjoint map takes values in the compact three-dimensional Lie algebra on the compact orbit. The angular pair from the central-character theorem, using that adjoint map and any fixed nonzero axis, gives a witness with the split variable. If the principal stabilizer is noncentral and its normalizer lies in a torus normalizer, use the stabilizer map followed by projection to the quotient of the compact rank-one group by its torus normalizer. This quotient parametrizes unoriented axes. The square of the projection onto a fixed axis is regular, and the angular calculation with the split variable applies. The invariant denominator clears the finitely many Laurent coefficient functions and preserves the marked orders just as above.

For a finite cyclic subgroup and for a binary dihedral subgroup other than $Q_8$, the torus used here is defined over $K$ by centralizing its characteristic cyclic subgroup, as in the split rank-one proof. The rank-one normalizer list now leaves only
$$
 (\mathrm{SU}_2/2O)\times\mathbb G_m,\qquad
 (\mathrm{SU}_2/2I)\times\mathbb G_m
$$
over the real closed ground field, with $\mathrm{SU}_2$ denoting the compact algebraic form. These are the usual compact embeddings of the binary groups. In fact a compact-orbit point with finite stabilizer has that stabilizer entirely in the compact rank-one group. In the polar decomposition of a complex stabilizer element, its positive part fixes the point since the point is norm-minimal and the norm squared along a positive one-parameter subgroup is strictly convex unless the generator fixes the point. A finite stabilizer has zero Lie algebra. The normalizer of a noncyclic finite subgroup is also compact: the positive part of a normalizing matrix centralizes the finite subgroup and is therefore trivial. The same polar argument turns complex conjugacy of these finite subgroups into compact conjugacy. Over other real closed fields these assertions about the finite subgroups follow by the finite-sign argument and quantifier elimination, using a finite list of points and the polynomial equations for conjugacy. The same denominator argument would lift witnesses from these two spaces to all remaining mixed cases.

For the exceptional products one can take the left circle quotient first. This pushes Haar to the uniform measure on the two-sphere modulo the corresponding rotation group. Adjoining inversion gives the reflection groups $B_3$ and $H_3$. Their invariant maps on the sphere give two regular coordinates on the quotient. On the open regular image the resulting density is proportional to $\Delta^{-1/2}$, where $\Delta$ is the square of the Jacobian determinant of the three basic invariants, written in invariant coordinates with the quadratic invariant set to one. Indeed the sphere-tangential Jacobian differs from the full Jacobian only by the length of the gradient of the quadratic invariant, constant on the sphere. The real fibers of the regular invariant map all have the same size. This gives a direct angular model for both open cases. It has an inverse square-root discriminant density, so the polynomial-density endpoint calculation does not apply to these coordinates. [Steinberg, Invariants of Finite Reflection Groups](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/canadian-journal-of-mathematics/article/invariants-of-finite-reflection-groups/0F630606F2C555E924905AA861997F03)

## Arithmetic specialization of marked moments

**Specialization lemma.** Let $E$ be a finitely generated extension of $\mathbb Q$, and let $b_m(\boldsymbol u)\in E[\boldsymbol u]$ be a sequence that is not eventually zero over $E(\boldsymbol u)$. Suppose it satisfies a scalar polynomial recurrence at every sufficiently large integer index, with nonzero first coefficient. Then one can specialize $\boldsymbol u$ in $E$ so that the sequence is still not eventually zero.

Write the recurrence as $\sum_{j=0}^r A_j(m,\boldsymbol u)b_{m+j}(\boldsymbol u)=0$, with $A_0\ne0$. Regard the coefficients of $A_0$ in the parameter variables as polynomials in $m$. Divide their common factor in $E[m]$ out of $A_0$ for the purpose of tracking its roots. Let $M$ exceed the fixed integer roots of that content and lie beyond the recurrence cutoff. Pick $m_0\ge M$ with $b_{m_0}$ a nonzero polynomial. Choose a parameter point $\boldsymbol a_0$ where $b_{m_0}$ is nonzero and the reduced first coefficient is a nonzero polynomial in $m$. Choose $\boldsymbol a_1$ where the latter does not vanish at any root of its evaluation at $\boldsymbol a_0$. Such a choice exists since the reduced coefficient has no common root over the algebraic closure in all its parameter coefficients. Restrict to the line through these two points. With line coordinate $u$, the reduced first coefficient has no constant root in $m$, since such a root would be common to the two evaluations. Also $b_{m_0}(u)$ is still nonzero. The zero-parameter case requires no specialization.

Factor the reduced first coefficient in $E(u)[m]$. Its linear factors have nonconstant roots $r_i(u)\in E(u)$. For each $i$ choose a different rational prime $p_i$ and an embedding of $E$ in $\mathbb Q_{p_i}$ under which there is an open ball of values of $u$ with $v_{p_i}(r_i(u))<0$. For a pole at infinity any such embedding and sufficiently large $p_i$-adic absolute values of $u$ work. For a finite pole $\alpha$, embed $E(\alpha)$ into $\mathbb Q_{p_i}$ and take values near $\alpha$. To justify the embeddings, for any given finitely generated field write it as a finite separable extension of a purely transcendental field over $\mathbb Q$. Specialize the transcendence basis to rational values where a monic minimal polynomial of a primitive element is defined and separable. A root of the specialized polynomial belongs to a number field. Infinitely many rational primes give it a simple root in $\mathbb Q_p$, by the prime-divisor theorem of Schur and Hensel's lemma. At each such prime one can perturb the transcendence basis to algebraically independent $p$-adic values sufficiently near the specialization, and Hensel's lemma lifts the same simple root. This gives an embedding of the original finitely generated field. In particular distinct primes can be chosen for the finitely many poles.

Let $\ell$ be a prime larger than the degree in $m$ of the first coefficient. If local conditions are needed, choose rational $a$ in all the prescribed balls by weak approximation, set $b=\prod_i p_i$, and take $c\in\mathbb Q^\times$ sufficiently small at all $p_i$. Put
$$
 u=a+\frac{c}{1+b t^\ell}.
$$
For any $t\in E$, each chosen valuation has value group $\mathbb Z$ under its embedding into $\mathbb Q_{p_i}$. Since $v_{p_i}(b)=1$, the two denominator summands have unequal valuations. The reciprocal denominator is integral at each chosen prime. Thus the substituted $u$ lies in all the required balls for every $t\in E$. The rational map $t\mapsto u$ has degree $\ell$. Every irreducible factor in $m$ of degree at least two remains irreducible after this substitution, because its degree in $m$ is coprime to the extension degree $\ell$. If there are no local conditions, use $u=t$. Hilbert irreducibility for the finitely generated field $E$ now allows a single specialization of $t$ preserving the degrees and irreducibility of all these factors. Exclude the finitely many parameter poles and zeros of $b_{m_0}$. [Fried–Jarden, Field Arithmetic, Chapter 13](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-031-28020-7)

At the selected parameter, the linear roots have negative valuation at an assigned prime and hence cannot be integers. The nonlinear irreducible factors have no roots in $E$. Therefore the original first coefficient has no integer zeros at $m\ge M$. If the specialized sequence were eventually zero, backward use of the recurrence would force $b_{m_0}=0$, contrary to the choice. This proves the specialization lemma.

### Application to arbitrary fields

**Theorem.** For every characteristic-zero field $K$, if a split rank-one subgroup acts nontrivially on an affine reductive homogeneous space $G/H$ with $G$ connected, then its Reynolds kernel is not Mathieu–Zhao. In particular this covers arbitrary stabilizers in split groups over every characteristic-zero field. It also covers nonsplit groups when an isotropic almost simple factor acts nontrivially.

Use the principal-orbit argument above and choose a finitely generated field of definition $E\subseteq K$ for the geometric data, the rational stabilizer map when needed, and the finite parameter families. The complex compact model supplies a parameter whose marked sequence is nonzero at infinitely many orders. In the noncentral case take the family $d(\boldsymbol u)$ in the invariant denominator ideal as in the uncountable-field argument. The relevant sequence is
$$
 b_m(\boldsymbol u)=\Lambda_X(d(\boldsymbol u)^{m+1})
                      \Lambda_{S/N}(Q_0P_0^m).
$$
Use this product sequence rather than the denominator moments in isolation, since the rank-one witness may have gaps. In the central case take a finite family of equivariant adjoint maps $Z(\boldsymbol u)$ containing the complex map used in that argument. The relevant sequence is $\Lambda_X(r(Z(\boldsymbol u))^{3m-1})$, since the adjoint constants $\beta_m$ are nonzero at every order. Both sequences are polynomial in the parameters at each order.

These sequences have the polynomial recurrence required by the specialization lemma. In the noncentral case they are mixed moments of the family $d(\boldsymbol u)\otimes P_0$ with multiplier $d(\boldsymbol u)\otimes Q_0$. In the central case they are mixed moments of $r(Z(\boldsymbol u))^3$ with multiplier $r(Z(\boldsymbol u))^2$, after shifting the index by one. Compute them on the acting group or the product of two acting groups by pullback. A connected group has an invariant volume form, and the normalized trace kills exact top forms against this volume form. Indeed the infinitesimal left translations give a global frame of vector fields preserving the volume form. An exact top form is a sum of derivatives along these fields times the volume form, and the normalized trace kills every such derivative. Apply the scalar exponent shift over $E(\boldsymbol u)$ and clear denominators to obtain identities in $E[\boldsymbol u]$ for all sufficiently large integer indices. [Loeser–Sabbah, §1.3](https://perso.pages.math.cnrs.fr/users/claude.sabbah/articles/loeser-sabbah91.pdf) · [Exponent shift proof](../../../unpublished/finite-tests-for-mixed-moments/main.tex)

The specialization lemma produces one parameter over $E$ whose marked sequence survives infinitely often. Purity is an algebraic rank-one identity for the entire family, so it also holds at that parameter. After extension to $K$ the pair still has exactly the same zero and nonzero orders. This replaces the uncountability assumption for the split rank-one branch. The argument does not need the two compact exceptional product cases.

### Scope of the remaining arithmetic question

The theorem addresses individual nonsplit homogeneous pairs whenever an isotropic almost simple factor acts nontrivially. It does not decide the case of a derived group anisotropic over the ground field that becomes noncompact at a real embedding. For example, the norm-one group of the rational quaternion algebra $(2,3)_{\mathbb Q}$ is anisotropic over $\mathbb Q$ but splits over $\mathbb R$. Its division property follows from the nontrivial Hilbert symbol at $3$. Compact square positivity at a real closure and the split rank-one argument over $\mathbb Q$ are both unavailable for that group.

## The split-identity classification and the toral nullcone over the ground field

**Corollary.** Let $K$ have characteristic zero. If $G^\circ$ is split reductive and $H^\circ$ is reductive, the normalized Reynolds kernel on the affine homogeneous space $G/H$ is Mathieu–Zhao exactly when $[G^\circ,G^\circ]\subseteq H$. This allows disconnected acting groups and stabilizers. For connected $G$ the same criterion characterizes equality of the pure set over $K$ with the geometric nullcone intersected with $K[G/H]$.

For the negative direction choose an active split almost simple factor on the $K$-defined open-and-closed component $G^\circ H/H$. A root $S$ acts nontrivially and the arithmetic specialization theorem applies. Extend the pair by zero to $G/H$. Its moments are multiplied by the reciprocal of the number of geometric components, by the component average over the algebraic closure. The positive Mathieu–Zhao direction follows by descent from the geometric toral case. In the connected case its pure functions lie in the geometric nullcone by the Laurent support criterion. In the negative case the fixed marked witness excludes geometric instability by the weight bound.

The toral branch admits a ground-field nullcone criterion also for nonsplit and disconnected groups. Set $D=[G^\circ,G^\circ]$ and assume $D\subseteq H$. Put $T=G^\circ/D$ and $M=X^*(T_{\overline K})$. Choose a finite Galois group $\Gamma$ through which the actions on $M$ and on the geometric components of $G/H$ factor. For each $\Gamma$-orbit $\alpha$ of components choose a representative $i$, with stabilizer $\Gamma_i$. Let $M_i\subset M$ be the character lattice of the quotient torus acting transitively on that component. Write $V_i=(M_i\otimes\mathbb Q)^{\Gamma_i}$ and $V=(M\otimes\mathbb Q)^\Gamma$. Then the pure set over $K$ equals the geometric nullcone intersected with $K[G/H]$ if and only if the following linear map is injective:
$$
 \bigoplus_\alpha V_i\longrightarrow V,\qquad
 (v_i)_\alpha\longmapsto \sum_\alpha\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}
                                   \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\gamma v_i .
$$

To prove this, extend to $\overline K$. The pure moments are the average of the component constant terms. Finite-trace separation in §7 of the complex paper gives purity on each component. Its weight support has convex hull avoiding zero by Duistermaat–van der Kallen. At a representative component the support is $\Gamma_i$-stable, so averaging its weights over $\Gamma_i$ still gives a support whose convex hull avoids zero. Choose on $V_i$ a rational linear functional strictly positive on that averaged support, with no constraint on an identically zero component. If the displayed map is injective, these functionals combine to a rational functional on $V$, hence to a $\Gamma$-invariant functional on $M\otimes\mathbb Q$ by averaging. It is positive on every original support weight. A suitable multiple is a cocharacter of $T$ and lifts up to a further multiple through the central torus of $G^\circ$. This contracts the function to zero.

Conversely, a nonzero rational relation in the displayed map can be multiplied to give integral weights $w_i\in M_i^{\Gamma_i}$. On each orbit with $w_i\ne0$, take the direct sum of the one-dimensional character spaces of weights $\gamma w_i$ on the corresponding geometric components. This space is stable under Galois, and a nonzero $K$-vector in its descended space has nonzero restriction to every component of that orbit. Use zero on all remaining orbits. This gives a pure $K$-function because every nonzero geometric component is a single nonzero weight. Its total weight support has zero in its convex hull by the relation, using equal coefficients within each orbit. No geometric cocharacter can contract it, so it lies outside the geometric nullcone. Conversely instability always implies purity because the moments are homogeneous invariant polynomials. If $T$ is split, the criterion simplifies to the sum of one embedded character lattice per Galois orbit of components. Over an algebraically closed field it recovers the component lattice criterion in the complex paper.

## A disconnected real closed boundary example

The connectedness hypothesis in the real closed classification matters. Let $K$ be real closed, let $C$ be its compact quaternion norm-one group, and take $G=C\times\mu_3$ with $H=1$. Its real identity component is compact. The coordinate algebra of $\mu_3$ decomposes as $K\times K(i)$, so $K[G]$ is the product of $K[C]$ and $K(i)[C_{K(i)}]$. The second factor geometrically accounts for the two nonreal components. Use the complex split rank-one pair with rational marked moments on that factor and extend both functions by zero on the first. The normalized trace is the three-component average after algebraic closure. Thus its pure moments vanish and its marked moments are $2/3$ times the original nonzero rational values. Consequently compactness of the connected real group is insufficient for the disconnected ground-field property.

## The split rank-one obstruction without rational points

Let $X$ be smooth affine and geometrically integral over a characteristic-zero field $K$, and let a connected reductive group $G$ act geometrically transitively. The normalized Reynolds trace $\Lambda_X$ is defined over $K$, since the invariants are the constants and the trivial isotypic projection commutes with scalar extension.

**Theorem.** If a split $\mathrm{SL}_2$ acts nontrivially through $G$, then $K[X]$ admits a pure phase and one fixed multiplier with nonzero marked moments at infinitely many orders. The conclusion applies whether or not $X(K)$ is nonempty.

The principal-orbit proof above works over an algebraic closure: after choosing a geometric point, $X$ is an affine homogeneous quotient there, and after passage to a complex model its compact orbit is Zariski dense. The same Kempf–Ness and Luna argument gives a dense invariant open $U$ with closed orbits and a common geometric stabilizer class. This open descends by uniqueness. The only step in the witness argument using a rational point was the choice of a stabilizer to define the normalizer quotient. Here is a replacement.

Write $S=\mathrm{SL}_{2,K}$, and let $L$ be a representative of the principal class over $\overline K$. Suppose $L$ is noncentral. Attach to it a subgroup $M(L)$ as follows.

- If $L^\circ$ is a torus, take $M(L)=N_S(L^\circ)$. For finite noncentral cyclic $L$, take the normalizer of its centralizer torus. For binary dihedral $L$ of order $4r$ with $r\ge3$, take the normalizer of the centralizer torus of its characteristic rotation subgroup. The latter is generated by the elements of order greater than four. These constructions are preserved by conjugation and by field automorphisms.
- For $L=Q_8,2T,2O,2I$ up to conjugacy, take $M(L)=N_S(L)$. The normalizers of $Q_8,2T,2O$ are of type $2O$ and the normalizer of $2I$ is of type $2I$. Indeed, the first three contain the standard $2O$ after conjugation, with $Q_8$ and $2T$ normal in $2O$. The centralizer of a finite noncyclic subgroup is the center, so each normalizer is finite. The finite subgroup list rules out a proper overgroup of $2O$ or $2I$: a binary cyclic or dihedral group cannot contain either, and $48$ does not divide $120$.

In every case $M(L)$ is conjugate to a standard $K$-defined subgroup $M_0$ of $S$, given by the split diagonal torus normalizer, the stabilizer of the binary octic $f_8$, or the stabilizer of the binary dodecic $f_{12}$. Each $M_0$ is self-normalizing. For the torus normalizer this follows by preserving its identity component. For the finite groups it is the maximality just proved. Conjugate $L$ over $\overline K$ so that $M(L)=M_0$. The map
$$
                         U_{\overline K}\dashrightarrow S_{\overline K}/M_0,
             \qquad S_x=sLs^{-1}\ \longmapsto\ sM_0
$$
is well defined because $N_S(L)\subseteq M_0$. Its graph is the image of $S_{\overline K}\times U^L$ by $(s,y)\mapsto(sy,sM_0)$ on geometric points. The closure of the graph is generically one-to-one over $U$, hence birational in characteristic zero. For any Galois automorphism $\sigma$, write $\sigma L=h Lh^{-1}$. Both $L$ and $\sigma L$ have attached group $M_0$, so $h\in N_S(M_0)=M_0$. Replacing $L$ by $\sigma L$ therefore leaves the map unchanged. This descends the rational map to $K$.

Pull back the corresponding $K$-defined quartic, octic or dodecic witness from $S/M_0$. The denominator ideal, the compact orbit construction of a denominator over $\mathbb C$, and the arithmetic specialization of marked moments above use only geometric homogeneity. In the central case, restriction onto a closed geometric orbit gives the adjoint covariant by base change of its intertwiner space, again without a $K$-point. The group calculation of the moment recurrence suffices after a finite field extension over which $X$ has a point. Such a field is obtained by adjoining the residue field of a closed point. There the orbit map from $G$ pulls back both regular functions and the trace, by uniqueness of the normalized invariant functional. A nonzero scalar recurrence for a sequence with coefficients in $E(\boldsymbol u)$ descends from a finite extension of $E(\boldsymbol u)$: expand its coefficients in a basis and take any component giving a nonzero first coefficient, then clear denominators. This gives exactly the input for the specialization lemma. In particular the proof retains one fixed multiplier over $K$.

### A component obstruction over any ground field

Let an affine variety $X$ be geometrically homogeneous under a group $G$ with reductive identity component. A Galois orbit of its geometric components gives an open-and-closed factor; its constant field $E$ is finite separable over $K$. The factor is a geometrically integral homogeneous $G^\circ_E$-variety $Y$ over $E$. In fact the geometric components are the orbits of $G^\circ_{\overline K}$, and base change of that factor to $\overline K$ is the family of components indexed by embeddings $E\hookrightarrow\overline K$.

The invariant trace on $X$ restricts on this factor to $\tfrac1r\operatorname{Tr}_{E/K}\circ\Lambda_Y$, where $r$ is the number of geometric components of $X$. This follows by extending scalars and averaging the normalized geometric-component traces. If $P,Q$ is a bad pair over $E$, take a $K$-basis $c_j$ of $E$. By nondegeneracy of the separable field-trace pairing, at each marked order where $b_m=\Lambda_Y(QP^m)\ne0$, at least one $\operatorname{Tr}_{E/K}(c_j b_m)$ is nonzero. One fixed $j$ works at infinitely many orders. Extend $P,c_jQ$ by zero using the idempotent of the component factor. Their pure and marked moments give a bad pair over $K$.

Thus one component factor with an active split rank-one action is sufficient, including factors defined over a proper extension $E$ and factors with no $E$-point. Over a real closed field the trace of each complex component factor is decided by the derived-action condition. A nontrivial derived action yields an active split rank-one subgroup over the complex constant field. If the derived action is trivial, the factor is toral, so its Reynolds kernel is Mathieu–Zhao by the Laurent theorem and finite-trace separation. For a real geometric component, an active split rank-one action suffices even if the real locus of that component is empty.

### A quaternion type of torsor

This theorem can also detect a split action from a second homogeneous presentation. If a connected group acts geometrically transitively while commuting with another connected group action, its normalized invariant trace is invariant under the commuting action by uniqueness. When both actions are geometrically transitive, the two normalized traces agree.

For example, let $D$ be a quaternion division algebra over any characteristic-zero field $K$ and put
$$
                         X=\operatorname{Isom}_{K\text{-alg}}(D,M_2(K)).
$$
Here $X$ is the scheme of algebra isomorphisms, with functor of points on $K$-algebras. It is affine, given by an invertible linear map subject to multiplication and unit equations. Over $\overline K$ the two quaternion algebras are matrix algebras, so $X_{\overline K}$ is a copy of $\mathrm{PGL}_2$. Precomposition with inverse inner automorphisms gives the geometrically transitive action of $\mathrm{PGL}_1(D)$. Postcomposition by inner automorphisms gives the commuting geometrically transitive split $\mathrm{PGL}_2$ action. The two traces coincide. There is also an explicit pair at every positive order. Take a nonzero reduced-trace-zero element $\xi\in D$, and form the traceless matrix $Z(\phi)=\phi(\xi)$ on $X$. The map is equivariant for the split adjoint action. Its invariant radius satisfies $r(Z)=-4\det Z=4\xi^2\in K^\times$, where $\xi^2$ is identified with a scalar. Evaluate the adjoint pair $P_{\rm ad}, Q_{\rm ad}$ at $Z$. The adjoint identities above give zero pure moments and marked moments $\beta_m(4\xi^2)^{3m-1}\ne0$ for every $m\ge1$. Thus the trace may fail for a homogeneous torsor of an anisotropic acting group even though the torsor has no $K$-point. For an example inside a disconnected quotient, take $G=\operatorname{Aut}_{K\text{-alg}}(D\times M_2(K))$ and let $H=\operatorname{Aut}_{K\text{-alg}}(M_2(K))$ act on the second factor only. There are two geometric components of $G$, according as an automorphism preserves the two central idempotents or exchanges them. Both components are defined over $K$. On the exchange component, restriction to the first factor identifies the quotient by $H$ with $X$: the free choice of the isomorphism from the second factor to the first is exactly the right $H$-orbit. The trace on $G/H$ fails by extending the pair from this component by zero.

### Compact adjoint torsors over real closed fields

**Theorem.** Let $K$ be real closed and $C$ be a connected adjoint semisimple group of compact type. For every $C$-torsor $X$, the normalized trace on $K[X]$ is Mathieu–Zhao exactly when $X(K)\ne\varnothing$.

Here a torsor is for the algebraic group $C$. Fix a point over $K(i)$, and use right torsor coordinates to identify $X_{K(i)}$ with $C_{K(i)}$. The $K$-structure is $\sigma_X(g)=c\,\sigma(g)$, where $\sigma$ is the $K$-conjugation on $C_{K(i)}$ and $c\,\sigma(c)=1$. The commuting group of right-$C$-equivariant automorphisms is the inner form $C_c$ with conjugation $\sigma_c=\operatorname{Int}(c)\sigma$, since left multiplication by $a$ transforms under $\sigma_X$ to left multiplication by $c\,\sigma(a)c^{-1}$. Its action is also simply transitive geometrically. An initial left torsor can be written as a right torsor using inversion in the acting group.

Suppose that the twist $C_c$ is compact. Compact real forms are conjugate by an inner automorphism (Adams–Taïbi, Theorem 3.7, cited above). Over an arbitrary real closed field this follows by transfer: negative definiteness of both Killing forms and the identity $\sigma_c=\operatorname{Int}(a)\sigma\operatorname{Int}(a)^{-1}$ with $a\in C(K(i))$ are finite algebraic conditions, and the real implication transfers. In applying transfer to a fixed presentation, the group and the two conjugations can be given by finitely many equations over a finitely generated ordered field, as in the real specialization argument above. The class of the cocycle is therefore trivial: adjointness gives $c=a\sigma(a)^{-1}$. The point with coordinate $a$ is $K$-rational. Conversely if the torsor has a point, it is isomorphic to $C$ and has strict square positivity. If it has no point, the commuting inner form $C_c$ is noncompact, so it has positive split rank and the split rank-one obstruction applies to its action on $X$. The traces agree by the commuting-action argument.

The same bad-pair argument works for a torsor of a nonadjoint compact semisimple group when its cocycle has nontrivial image in the adjoint cohomology set. In particular the point criterion extends to compact semisimple groups with center of odd order. Indeed, if the adjoint image is trivial, the class comes from the center, on which compact conjugation fixes every geometric point. For a center of odd order the identity is the only cocycle of order dividing two. For an adjoint-trivial class one can change torsor coordinates so the cocycle is central, and the commuting inner form is compact. That argument leaves central twists, the norm-one group of $D=(2,3)_{\mathbb Q}$, and the two compact exceptional scalar products open.

### Self-normalizing compact stabilizers

If $C$ has compact type over a real closed field and $L$ is a geometric reductive stabilizer with $N_{C_{\overline K}}(L)=L$, any $C$-homogeneous $K$-form of $C_{\overline K}/L$ has a $K$-point. To see this, first construct a $K$-subgroup in the conjugacy class of $L$. Over $\mathbb R$, a compact form of the reductive subgroup $L$ conjugates into $C(\mathbb R)$ by Adams–Taïbi, Theorem 3.7. Its Zariski closure is the corresponding conjugate of $L$, defined over $\mathbb R$. Equivalently there is a conjugate of $L$ preserved by compact conjugation. That condition is finite algebraic data. Embed into a general linear group, use the matrix entries and the inverse determinant as affine coordinates, and choose equations for $L$. Conjugating the equations and conjugating their coefficients gives polynomials of bounded degree. Membership in the ideal of $L$ in that degree range is membership in a fixed finite-dimensional linear subspace, which commutes with scalar extension. Impose this condition for both inclusions. Then the real-closed transfer argument gives a $K$-defined conjugate $L_0$. Over $\overline K$ a $C$-equivariant automorphism of $C/L_0$ lies in $N_C(L_0)/L_0=1$, so the geometric $C$-equivariant isomorphism from $C/L_0$ to the given form is unique and descends. The identity coset gives a $K$-point, and compact square positivity proves the Mathieu property. In particular, compact rank-one homogeneous forms with stabilizer $2O$, $2I$, or a torus normalizer introduce no additional no-point cases.

## A radial Laurent ansatz fails at order two for inverse square-root densities

This sharpens the first-moment test for the two compact exceptional products. Let $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^d$ be a bounded open set, $d\ge1$, and let a polynomial $f$ be positive on $\Omega$ and zero on its boundary. Suppose that $f^{-1/2}$ is integrable on $\Omega$. Use this density, up to normalization, followed by constant term in $t$. For positive integers $b,N$ and any integer $a$ set
$$
p(t,x)=t^{-1}(1+t)^a f\!\left((1+t)^b x\right)^N .
$$
If $\Lambda(p)=0$, then $\Lambda(p^2)>0$. The same holds if the dilation is about any fixed center, by translating coordinates. Taking $a\ge0$ gives a regular Laurent ansatz. The calculation for negative $a$ uses only expansion at $t=0$.

Let $V=x\cdot\nabla$ and put $J_k=\int_\Omega f^{k-1/2}dx$, $A_k=kd/(k-1/2)$. Integration by parts gives
$$
I_k=\int_\Omega V(f^k) f^{-1/2}dx=-A_k J_k .
$$
The primitive $f^{k-1/2}$ vanishes on the boundary. For $k\ge2$, another integration by parts gives
$$
\int_\Omega V^2(f^k) f^{-1/2}dx
 =-d I_k+\frac{k}{2}\int_\Omega f^{k-5/2}(Vf)^2dx
 \ \ge\ A_k^2 J_k .
$$
The inequality is Cauchy–Schwarz with weights $f^{k-5/2}$ and $f^{k-1/2}$, using $I_k/k=\int_\Omega f^{k-3/2}Vf$. For rigor near the boundary, replace $f^\gamma$ by $\max(f^\gamma-\epsilon^\gamma,0)$ extended by zero outside $\Omega$, for $\gamma=k-1/2$ or $k-3/2$. Their gradients converge in $L^1$, because $f^{-1/2}$ is integrable and $\nabla f$ is bounded. This justifies both integrations by parts.

For the first moment the constant-term coefficient is $(a-b A_N)J_N$, so vanishing forces $a=b A_N$. Put $s=\log(1+t)$ and
$$
H(s)=e^{2as}\int_\Omega f(e^{bs}x)^{2N} f(x)^{-1/2}dx .
$$
The required second moment is proportional to $[t^2]H(\log(1+t))=(H''(0)-H'(0))/2$. For $C=2a-b A_{2N}$ the two identities above give $H'(0)=C J_{2N}$ and $H''(0)\ge C^2 J_{2N}$. With the first moment zero,
$$
 C=b(2A_N-A_{2N})
   =\frac{2bN^2d}{(N-1/2)(2N-1/2)}>bd\ge1.
$$
Thus the second moment is strictly positive. All differentiations at zero concern a finite polynomial in $e^{bs}$ integrated against a fixed integrable density.

For the discriminants of $B_3$ and $H_3$ the invariant-coordinate image has $d=2$ and the required law. For example, in the $B_3$ model use $(p_2,p_3)=(xy+yz+zx,xyz)$ for squared coordinates of the unit sphere with $x+y+z=1$. Up to scale the discriminant polynomial is
$$
 f(p_2,p_3)=p_3(p_2^2-4p_2^3-4p_3-27p_3^2+18p_2p_3).
$$
For $(a,b,N)=(8,3,2)$, the first moment vanishes, and the second normalized moment is the positive number
$$
 \frac{1103693348864}{101824201498122560376553308675}.
$$
This can be checked by expanding $f^4$ in $p_2,p_3$ and weighting a monomial $p_2^i p_3^j$ by $\binom{16+3(i+j)}{2}$. For the remaining integration use $\mathbb E[x^\alpha y^\beta z^\gamma]=(\tfrac12)_\alpha(\tfrac12)_\beta(\tfrac12)_\gamma/(\tfrac32)_{\alpha+\beta+\gamma}$. This obstruction concerns a single radial ansatz. A pure higher power of a nonpure seed, nonradial deformations, and phases outside the two basic invariant coordinates require separate arguments.

## Pure quadratic phases on norm quadrics

Let $K$ have characteristic zero, let $q$ be a nondegenerate quadratic form on $K^n$, $n\ge2$, and let $X=\{q(x)=1\}$. Write $q(x,y)$ for the associated bilinear form, with $q(x,x)=q(x)$, and let $\Lambda$ be the normalized $\mathrm{SO}(q)$-trace on $K[X]$. Any phase of ambient degree at most two can be written uniquely on $X$ as
$$
              P=q(x,A x)+q(b,x),\qquad A=A^*,\quad b\in K^n,
$$
by absorbing the constant into a scalar multiple of $q(x)$. A quadratic and a linear part cannot cancel on $X$ because of the involution $x\mapsto-x$. The uniqueness of the quadratic part follows by scalar extension and rescaling the dense set $q\ne0$ to $X$.

**Theorem.** The first $4n$ positive moments of $P$ vanish if and only if all its positive moments vanish. This holds exactly when $A$ is nilpotent and $q(b,A^j b)=0$ for every $j\ge0$. For such a phase and a polynomial multiplier $Q$ one has $\Lambda(QP^m)=0$ for all $m>\lfloor n/2\rfloor\deg Q$. This coefficient is sharp in each dimension. In particular an anisotropic norm quadric has no nonzero pure phase of ambient degree at most two.

### The coefficient identity

Normalize the following series at the origin.
$$
\begin{aligned}
 d(u)&=\det(I-2uA), & D(u)&=d(u)^{-1/2},\\
 B(u)&=q\!\left(b,(I-2uA)^{-1}b\right), & \phi(u)&=1+u^2 B(u).
\end{aligned}
$$
The formal Gaussian moment functional with covariance $q^{-1}$ satisfies
$$
 {\cal G}\!\left(e^{u q(x,Ax)+v q(b,x)}\right)
       = D(u)\exp\!\left(\frac{v^2}{2}B(u)\right).
$$
This follows by diagonalizing $q$ over an algebraic closure and completing the square. Equivalently one can first verify each coefficient by convergent Gaussian integration near zero for positive definite real $q$ and real symmetric $A$, then use polynomial continuation in the matrix and vector coefficients. The sphere–Gaussian radial relation on a homogeneous polynomial of degree $2j$ is
$$
                  {\cal G}(F)=2^j(n/2)_j\,\Lambda(F).
$$
The relation and vanishing in odd degree both follow from ordinary polar integration in the standard positive model and transport to any $q$ after scalar extension.

In $P^m$, terms with $2k$ linear factors have total degree $2(m-k)$, and terms with an odd number of linear factors have zero integral. Put $\nu=n/2$. The Gaussian coefficient of $u^{m-2k}v^{2k}$, divided by its radial factor and multiplied by $2^m(\nu)_m$, contributes $\binom{m+\nu-1}{k}[u^{m-2k}]D(u)B(u)^k$ to the scaled moment $2^m(\nu)_m\Lambda(P^m)/m!$. Summing gives
$$
       2^m(\nu)_m\frac{\Lambda(P^m)}{m!}
                 =[u^m]D(u)\phi(u)^{m+\nu-1}.                   \tag{Q1}
$$
Write $u=z\phi(u)$ for the formal inverse of $z=u/\phi(u)$. Formal residue substitution gives
$$
 \sum_{m\ge0}z^m[u^m]D(u)\phi(u)^{m+\nu-1}
        =\left.\frac{D(u)\phi(u)^\nu}{\phi(u)-u\phi'(u)}
          \right|_{u=z\phi(u)}.                               \tag{Q2}
$$
Indeed the right side times $dz/z^{m+1}$ pulls back to $D(u)\phi(u)^{m+\nu-1}du/u^{m+1}$. The denominator on the right has constant coefficient one. Consequently purity is equivalent to the rational-function identity
$$
              \phi(u)^n=d(u)\big(\phi(u)-u\phi'(u)\big)^2.       \tag{Q3}
$$
Squaring loses no information because both formal sides of the unsquared identity have constant coefficient one.

### The pole argument

The first moment is $\operatorname{tr}(A)/n$, so purity gives $\operatorname{tr}(A)=0$. If $\phi$ has a finite zero $a$ of multiplicity $e>0$, then $a\ne0$ and $\phi-u\phi'$ has order $e-1$ there. Equation (Q3) gives
$$
              \operatorname{ord}_a d=(n-2)e+2\ge n.
$$
Since $\deg d\le n$, all eigenvalues of $A$ would equal the nonzero number $1/(2a)$, contradicting its zero trace. Hence $\phi$ has no finite zeros and $R=1/\phi$ is a polynomial with $R(0)=1$. Because the denominator of $B$ divides $d$, the same holds for the denominator of $\phi$, and $R$ divides $d$. The rational identity becomes
$$
               d^{-1}=(R+uR')^2 R^{n-4}.                       \tag{Q4}
$$
For $n\ge4$ the right side is a polynomial, so both sides are constant and $R$ is constant as well. For $n=3$ use $1=(d/R)(R+uR')^2$ to reach the same conclusion. For $n=2$, a root $a$ of $R$ has $a\ne0$ and the multiplicity of $R+uR'$ there is one less than that of $R$. Thus $d(R+uR')^2=R^2$ forces $\operatorname{ord}_a d=2$, again contradicting zero trace. This proves $R=1$, and then $d=1$ by (Q4). The characteristic polynomial implies nilpotence of $A$, and $B=0$ gives $q(b,A^j b)=0$ by expansion of the resolvent. Conversely those two conditions give $D=\phi=1$ in (Q1).

For the finite test write $h=d+u^2 q(b,\operatorname{adj}(I-2uA)b)$, so $\phi=h/d$. Set $U=hd-u(h'd-hd')$. Then $\deg h\le n+1$, $\deg d\le n$ and $\deg U\le2n$; the potential term of degree $2n+1$ cancels because its coefficient has the factor $1-(n+1)+n$. If the first $M$ moments vanish, (Q2) gives $h^n/(U^2d^{n-3})=1+O(u^{M+1})$. Its logarithmic derivative is
$$
                 n\,h'/h-2\,U'/U-(n-3)\,d'/d=O(u^M).
$$
Over the denominator $hUd$ the numerator has degree at most $4n-1$. The only possible term of degree $4n$ requires all three degrees to be maximal and cancels because $n(n+1)-2(2n)-(n-3)n=0$. For $M=4n$ the numerator therefore vanishes identically. The ratio has constant coefficient one, proving (Q3) and purity.

If $q$ is anisotropic, any nonzero selfadjoint nilpotent gives a nonzero isotropic vector. Choose $v$ with $A^jv\ne0$ for the last nonzero power of $A$, for which $2j$ is at least the nilpotence index. Then $q(A^jv,A^jv)=q(v,A^{2j}v)=0$. Thus $A=0$, and $q(b,b)=0$ gives $b=0$. The result applies at any nonzero norm level by replacing $q$ by its scalar rescaling. For the open arithmetic example $\mathrm{SL}_1((2,3)_{\mathbb Q})$ every nonzero phase of ambient degree at most two is detected by one of its first sixteen positive moments. The same holds for the quaternion norm-minus-one central torsor over a real closed field.

### The sharp marked bound

Let $r\ge1$ be the nilpotence index of $A$ and let $\ell$ be the length of the cyclic chain of $b$ under $A$ (zero if $b=0$). Its span is totally isotropic since $q(A^i b,A^j b)=q(b,A^{i+j}b)=0$. Thus $\ell\le\lfloor n/2\rfloor$. For a homogeneous marker $Q$ of degree $s$, the generating series
$$
           {\cal G}\!\left(Qe^{u q(x,Ax)+v q(b,x)}\right)
$$
is the Gaussian pairing polynomial of $Q$ with covariance $(I-2uA)^{-1}q^{-1}$ and mean $v(I-2uA)^{-1}b$. The unmarked exponential factor is one by purity. Each pair contributes at most $r-1$ to the total degree in $(u,v)$, and each singleton contributes at most $\ell$. Hence the total degree is bounded by $s\max((r-1)/2,\ell)\le s\lfloor n/2\rfloor$. The sphere–Gaussian radial relation transports each zero coefficient separately, since $Qq(x,Ax)^j q(b,x)^k$ is homogeneous for each $j,k$. This proves the claimed marked bound after summing the homogeneous pieces of $Q$.

For sharpness put $k=\lfloor n/2\rfloor$. Take a split $2k$-dimensional block with basis $e_1,\ldots,e_{2k}$, pairings $q(e_i,e_j)=\delta_{i+j,2k+1}$, and $A e_i=e_{i-1}$ with $A e_1=0$. Add an orthogonal line with $A=0$ if $n$ is odd. Take $b=e_k$ and $Q=x_1^s$, $s\ge1$. The means have top singleton term $v(2u)^{k-1}$ in coordinate $x_1$. Each pair has degree at most $2k-1$, so the only term of total degree $ks$ in the marked Gaussian series comes from $s$ top singleton terms. Its sphere–Gaussian rescaling gives
$$
                   \Lambda(QP^{ks})=\frac{(ks)!}{2^s(n/2)_{ks}}\ne0.
$$
Thus the same coefficient $k$ is necessary in every dimension, including for any prescribed marker degree $s$.

For finite cross-checks, direct sphere monomial integration agrees with (Q1) for mixed diagonal phases in dimensions two through six at orders one through six. Direct hyperbolic Gaussian pairings followed by radial rescaling reproduce the sharp value and next-order vanishing in dimensions two through eight for marker degrees one and two. The unrestricted conclusions above follow from the identities and pole argument.

## Homogeneous phases with a powered linear factor on anisotropic norm quadrics

Keep the same $K$, $q$, $X$, and $\Lambda$. Suppose $q$ is anisotropic and $2\le n\le4$.

**Theorem.** If $L$ is a nonzero linear form, $Q$ is a nonzero homogeneous quadratic polynomial, and $r\ge1$, the phase $L^r Q$ on $X$ has a nonzero positive moment. In particular no nonzero homogeneous cubic with a $K$-linear factor is pure on the quaternion norm-one quadric or its compact norm-minus-one twist.

Write $Q=q(x,Ax)$ with $A$ selfadjoint and $L=q(b,x)$ with $b\ne0$. The operator $A$ is semisimple. Its nilpotent Jordan part is defined over $K$ and is a polynomial in $A$, since $K$ is perfect. Thus it is selfadjoint and vanishes by the anisotropy argument in the preceding section. As $Q\ne0$, this also shows that $d(u)=\det(I-2uA)$ is nonconstant.

Put
$$
       \kappa(u)=\frac{q(b,(I-2uA)^{-1}b)}{q(b,b)},\qquad
       \theta(u)=\frac{1}{\kappa(u)},\qquad
       D(u)=d(u)^{-1/2}.
$$
The denominator $q(b,b)$ is nonzero. For $rm$ even, the Gaussian moment of $L^{rm}$ weighted by $e^{uQ}$ is
$$
                 {\cal G}(L^{rm}e^{uQ})
                      ={\cal G}(L^{rm})D(u)\kappa(u)^{rm/2}.
$$
This follows from the centered Gaussian with tilted covariance $(I-2uA)^{-1}q^{-1}$, or by extracting coefficients in the Gaussian identity above. The unweighted factor is nonzero. Also $L^{rm}Q^m$ is homogeneous, so its sphere moment differs from its Gaussian moment by a nonzero scalar. Therefore, writing $\psi(u)=\theta(u)^{-r/2}$, purity is equivalent to
$$
                          [u^m]D(u)\psi(u)^m=0
               \quad\text{for all }m\ge1\text{ with }rm\text{ even}.        \tag{L1}
$$
The other orders vanish by sign. Let $u(z)$ be the formal inverse of $z=u/\psi(u)$. The same formal residue substitution gives
$$
  \sum_{m\ge0} z^m [u^m]D(u)\psi(u)^m
         =\Psi(u(z)),\qquad
     \Psi(u)=\frac{D(u)}{1+(r/2)u\,\theta'(u)/\theta(u)}.                 \tag{L2}
$$

We may work over $\mathbb C$ by embedding a finitely generated field of definition for the finitely many coefficients. Since $A$ is semisimple, its eigenspace decomposition gives
$$
                    \kappa(u)=\sum_{\lambda}\frac{c_\lambda}{1-2\lambda u},
                        \qquad \sum_\lambda c_\lambda=1.
$$
The sum is over distinct eigenvalues, including zero if present. Thus $\kappa$ is bounded at infinity and $\theta(u)\sim C u^j$ there, with $j\ge0$ and $C\ne0$. The denominator of $\Psi$ tends to $1+rj/2\ne0$. Since $d$ is nonconstant, $\Psi^2\to0$ at infinity. If $r$ is even, (L1) and (L2) give $\Psi=1$, a contradiction.

Suppose $r$ is odd. Let $s=z^2$ and consider the rational map
$$
                            s= u^2\theta(u)^r .
$$
For a generic $s$, its distinct inverse branches include $u(z)$ and $u(-z)$, both approaching zero as $s\to0$. Equation (L1) gives $\Psi(u(z))+\Psi(u(-z))=2$. Consequently, putting $T(u)=\Psi(u)^2$, one has
$$
          (4-T(u(z))-T(u(-z)))^2=4T(u(z))T(u(-z)).                  \tag{L3}
$$
Let a graph on the generic roots have as edges the Galois orbit over $\mathbb C(s)$ of this unordered pair. The roots are transitively permuted: for the map $N(u)/M(u)$ in coprime polynomials the equation $N(u)-sM(u)=0$ is irreducible, since in $\mathbb C[u,s]$ a factor independent of $s$ would divide both $N$ and $M$. The graph therefore has the same positive degree at each vertex. Equation (L3) holds on every edge because $T$ is rational.

As $s\to\infty$ each branch approaches infinity in $u$ or a finite pole $a$ of $\theta$. Near a finite pole the denominator of $\Psi$ has a simple pole because $a\ne0$. Therefore $T$ tends to zero there unless $a$ is also a root of $d$ of multiplicity at least two. The only possible such exceptional pole has multiplicity two in $d$, and there can be at most one. More precisely, if $d(a)=0$ with multiplicity at least two, the corresponding nonzero eigenvalue $\lambda=1/(2a)$ has multiplicity at least two. For $\kappa$ to vanish at $a$ its term $c_\lambda/(1-2\lambda u)$ must be absent. In dimension at most four there are at most two other distinct eigenvalues. If there is only one contributing term then $\kappa$ has no finite zero. If there are two, their combined numerator has degree at most one. An exceptional $a$ therefore has multiplicity two in $d$, is the only finite zero of $\kappa$, and that zero is simple. In that case $T$ has a fixed finite nonzero limit at $a$.

If no exceptional pole exists, every vertex has limiting value zero for $T$, contradicting (L3). Otherwise, label vertices by whether the branch tends to $a$ or to infinity. There are $r$ branches at $a$ and $2+rj$ branches at infinity, by the respective pole orders of the rational map. The limiting value of $T$ is zero at the latter vertices. By (L3) their neighbors must have limiting value four. Consequently no two neighbors can both be from the former class, since $(4-4-4)^2\ne4\cdot4\cdot4$. The graph is bipartite and regular with positive degree, so its two classes have equal size. This gives $r=2+rj$, impossible for odd $r$. Thus the phase is not pure.

The dimension bound enters only in counting possible exceptional poles. The even-$r$ argument works in every dimension, and so does the odd-$r$ argument when all roots of $d$ are simple. For example, on the rational quaternion form $\langle1,-2,-3,6\rangle$, the choice $A=\operatorname{diag}(1,0,2,2)$, $b=e_1+e_2$ gives $\theta=(1-2u)/(1-4u)$. It realizes the exceptional-pole case, with two branches at infinity and $r$ at $1/4$. The conclusion uses the full identity (L3), not an extrapolation from a moment prefix.

### The geometrically reducible cubic boundary in dimension four

A homogeneous cubic using at most three linear coordinates over $K$ cannot be pure on an anisotropic norm quadric. One can identify these coordinates with a subspace using $q$. The restricted form is nondegenerate by anisotropy, and orthogonal decomposition reduces the Gaussian moments to the corresponding lower-dimensional Gaussian. Homogeneity and the radial identity transfer purity both ways. In three variables the [Gaussian boundary paper, Theorem 1.3](../../../results/gaussian-boundary/manuscript.tex) says that a pure homogeneous cubic is one-sided for an orthogonal circular frame. Its exceptional phase has nonzero pieces of degrees one, two, and three and cannot be made homogeneous by a linear change of variables. In two variables, a homogeneous polynomial of degree $d$ in split circular coordinates $Z,W$ is $W^dF(Z/W)$. Its Gaussian moment at order $m$, when $dm$ is even, is a nonzero scalar times the middle coefficient $[z^{dm/2}]F(z)^m$. Apply the Laurent theorem to $z^{-d} F(z^2)$ to conclude one-sidedness; for odd $dm$ the middle coefficient is zero automatically. The one-variable nonvanishing follows from the even Gaussian moments.

The existence of a one-sided orthogonal frame descends from a complex model to the algebraic closure of the field of definition, because the frame condition and the vanishing of the forbidden coefficients are finite polynomial conditions. Hence the phase is geometrically unstable for the special orthogonal group of the restricted form. [Kempf, §4, Theorem 7 in the transcribed preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.02890) supplies a contracting cocharacter over the perfect field $K$. An anisotropic orthogonal group has no such cocharacter, since any nonzero weight vector of a split cocharacter would be isotropic for the quadratic form.

In dimension four a geometrically reducible cubic either has a $K$-linear factor or is an irreducible product over $K$ of three Galois-conjugate geometric linear factors. Indeed, for a line times an irreducible conic the unique line descends, and for three lines any singleton orbit descends. In the remaining case the span of the geometric linear factors descends as a subspace of dimension at most three. The powered-linear-factor theorem handles the first case and the lower-dimensional Gaussian argument handles the second. Thus a nonzero pure homogeneous cubic in four anisotropic variables must be geometrically irreducible and use all four variables. This applies in particular to the norm-one quadric for $(2,3)_{\mathbb Q}$.

### Central compact twists and sign

For a central involution $c$ of a compact group $C$ over a real closed field, let the torsor be described over $K(i)$ by $g\mapsto c\,\sigma(g)$. On its coordinate ring the central translation by $c$ gives two eigenspaces. A nonzero function from either eigenspace has nonzero second trace. To see this over $\mathbb R$, identify the complexified torsor with $C_{\mathbb C}$ and evaluate a real-defined function $P$ on the original compact real form. At such a point $g$ the torsor descent condition gives $\overline{P(g)}=P(cg)$. Thus the values are real in the positive eigenspace and purely imaginary in the negative eigenspace. In both cases their squares have fixed sign and the normalized compact Haar integral is nonzero by density. The real-closed specialization used earlier transfers this statement for any given algebraic phase. Any nonzero pure phase on a central compact twist must therefore have both signs. For the norm-minus-one quaternion torsor this means that it must mix even and odd ambient degrees, and the quadratic classification forces degree at least three as well.
