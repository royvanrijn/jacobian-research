\begin{abstract}
We give an explicit counterexample to the Special Image Conjecture in
three contraction pairs.  In
\(\C[\tau,w,v,t,z,y]\), let
\[
 f=\tau(t-y)(wz+vt),\qquad g=y.
\]
For the standard contraction map \(\Eop\), we prove for every \(m\geq1\)
that
\[
 \Eop(f^m)=0,\qquad
 [t]\Eop(gf^m)=(-1)^{m-1}(m+1)!\,m!.
\]
Thus every positive power of \(f\) lies in the image of the operators
\(\partial_t-\tau,\partial_z-w,\partial_y-v\), whereas
\(gf^m\notin\M_3\) for every \(m\geq1\).  The polynomial \(f\) has
four terms and the multiplier is linear.  Its two-pair dehomogenization is
exactly Christopher D. Long's counterexample to the Mathieu conjecture for
\(SU(2)\); the present construction is its one-pair bihomogeneous lift.
We also prove an explicit two-pair identity of bidegree \((4,4)\).
Together with the known one-pair theorem, it shows that the minimum
failing number of contraction pairs is exactly two.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem and the construction}

For \(r\geq1\), write
\[
 R_r=\C[\zeta_1,\ldots,\zeta_r,z_1,\ldots,z_r],
 \qquad
 \M_r=\sum_{i=1}^r(\partial_{z_i}-\zeta_i)R_r.
\]
The Special Image Conjecture asserts that \(\M_r\) is a Mathieu
subspace: if \(h^m\in\M_r\) for every \(m\geq1\), then
\(qh^m\in\M_r\) for every \(q\in R_r\) and all sufficiently large
\(m\); see Zhao~\cite{Zhao2010} and van den Essen, Wright, and
Zhao~\cite{EWZ2011}.

Define the polynomial-valued contraction map
\[
 \Eop_r:R_r\longrightarrow\C[z_1,\ldots,z_r],
 \qquad
 \Eop_r(\zeta^\alpha z^\beta)=\partial_z^\alpha z^\beta.
\]

The difficulty is cancellation at every power: testing a long finite list
of contractions cannot establish the Mathieu hypothesis. Our construction
starts from Long's two-pair factorial-moment cancellation and adds a
homogenizing contraction pair. The new factorial factor is common to all
surviving terms, so the cancellation persists. A fixed multiplier shifts
one exponent and leaves a nonzero boundary term. This explains why a
four-term polynomial can work at every order.

At the first order the mechanism is already visible:
\[
 \Eop_3(f)=0,\qquad \Eop_3(yf)=2t-y.
\]
The proof below replaces this example by two binomial identities valid
for every positive exponent. Section~\ref{sec:two-pair} then gives a
separate two-pair construction, using a higher-degree polynomial.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:kernel}
For every \(r\geq1\), one has \(\M_r=\ker\Eop_r\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For \(h\in R_r\),
\[
 \Eop_r(\partial_{z_i}h)
 =\partial_{z_i}\Eop_r(h)
 =\Eop_r(\zeta_i h).
\]
Thus \(\Eop_r((\partial_{z_i}-\zeta_i)h)=0\), so
\(\M_r\subseteq\ker\Eop_r\).  Conversely,
\[
 \zeta^\alpha z^\beta
 -\zeta^{\alpha-e_i}\partial_{z_i}z^\beta
 =-(\partial_{z_i}-\zeta_i)(\zeta^{\alpha-e_i}z^\beta)
 \in\M_r\qquad(\alpha_i>0).
\]
Induction on the total dual degree therefore gives, for every monomial,
\[
 \zeta^\alpha z^\beta
 \equiv\partial_z^\alpha z^\beta
 =\Eop_r(\zeta^\alpha z^\beta)
 \pmod{\M_r}.
\]
By linearity, \(p\equiv\Eop_r(p)\pmod{\M_r}\).  Since \(\Eop_r\) is
the identity on \(\C[z_1,\ldots,z_r]\), \(\Eop_r(p)=0\) implies
\(p\in\M_r\).
\end{proof}

Use the pairs
\[
 (\tau,t),\qquad(w,z),\qquad(v,y)
\]
and put
\[
 \M_3=(\partial_t-\tau)R_3+
       (\partial_z-w)R_3+
       (\partial_y-v)R_3.
\]

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
Let
\[
 \boxed{\quad
 f=\tau(t-y)(wz+vt),\qquad g=y.
 \quad}
\]
Then, for every \(m\geq1\),
\[
 \Eop_3(f^m)=0,\qquad
 [t]\Eop_3(gf^m)=(-1)^{m-1}(m+1)!\,m!\neq0.
\]
In particular, \(\Eop_3(gf^m)\neq0\).  Consequently
\(f^m\in\M_3\) and \(gf^m\notin\M_3\) for every \(m\geq1\).
\end{theorem}

In expanded form,
\[
\boxed{\;
 f=\tau vt^2-\tau vty+\tau wtz-\tau wyz.
\;}
\]
This displays the advertised four terms directly.

\section{Proof}

First omit the pair \((\tau,t)\).  On
\(\C[W,Z,V,Y]\), define
\[
 \mathcal F(W^aZ^bV^cY^d)
 =\delta_{a,b}\delta_{c,d}\,a!\,c!
\]
and set
\[
 P=(1-Y)(WZ+V),\qquad Q=Y.
\]
Expanding the second factor gives
\[
 P^m=\sum_{k=0}^m\binom mk
 W^{m-k}Z^{m-k}V^k(1-Y)^m.
\]
For a nonzero contraction, the \(V,Y\) pair selects
\([Y^k](1-Y)^m=(-1)^k\binom mk\).  Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pure}
 \mathcal F(P^m)
 =\sum_{k=0}^m(-1)^k\binom mk^2(m-k)!\,k!
 =m!\sum_{k=0}^m(-1)^k\binom mk=0.
\end{equation}
After multiplication by \(Q=Y\), the \(V,Y\) pair instead selects
\([Y^k]Y(1-Y)^m=(-1)^{k-1}\binom m{k-1}\) for \(1\leq k\leq m\).
Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixed}
 \begin{aligned}
 \mathcal F(YP^m)
 &=m!\sum_{k=1}^m(-1)^{k-1}\binom m{k-1}\\
 &=(-1)^{m-1}m!.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Homogenize in the coordinate variables \(Z,Y\), treating \(W,V\) as
coefficient, or dual, variables:
\[
 \widetilde P(t,w,z,v,y)
 =t^2P\left(w,\frac zt,v,\frac yt\right)
 =(t-y)(wz+vt).
\]
The polynomial \(f^m\) has equal total dual and coordinate degrees,
so its contraction is a scalar: survival requires equality of each pair
of exponents. Each factor of \(P\) has total \(W,V\)-degree one.  Every monomial of
\(P^m\) that survives \(\mathcal F\) therefore has total
\((Z,Y)\)-degree \(m\), matching its total \(W,V\)-degree.  Its
coordinate homogenization contains
\(t^{2m-m}=t^m\), exactly matching the power \(\tau^m\).
The new contraction contributes \(m!\), and
\eqref{eq:pure} gives
\[
 \Eop_3(f^m)=m!\mathcal F(P^m)=0.
\]

Multiplication by \(Y\) selects terms of \(P^m\) with total
\((Z,Y)\)-degree \(m-1\).  Its homogenization contains
\(t^{2m-(m-1)}=t^{m+1}\).  Contracting against \(\tau^m\)
leaves one power of \(t\) and contributes \((m+1)!\).  By
\eqref{eq:mixed},
\[
 [t]\Eop_3(yf^m)
 =(m+1)!\mathcal F(YP^m)
 =(-1)^{m-1}(m+1)!\,m!\neq0.
\]
Lemma~\ref{lem:kernel} completes the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.

\section{Relation with the
  \texorpdfstring{\(xz\)- and \(SU(2)\)}{xz- and SU(2)} counterexamples}

Write the coordinate functions on \(SU(2)\) as
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}a&c\\ b&d\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Under the substitution
\[
 W=a,\qquad Z=d,\qquad V=b,\qquad Y=-c,
\]
the two-pair seed and multiplier become
\[
 P=(1-Y)(WZ+V)\longmapsto(1+c)(ad+b),\qquad
 Q=Y\longmapsto-c.
\]
This is precisely Long's \(SU(2)\) counterexample~\cite[Theorem 4.2]{Long2026}.
Indeed normalized Haar integration satisfies
\[
 \int_{SU(2)}a^r b^s c^t d^u\,dg
 =(-1)^s\delta_{r,u}\delta_{s,t}
   \frac{r!\,s!}{(r+s+1)!}.
\]
Because every monomial of \(P^m\) has total \(W,V\)-degree \(m\),
this formula gives
\[
 \mathcal F(P^m)=(m+1)!\int_{SU(2)}((1+c)(ad+b))^m\,dg
\]
and the analogous identity with multipliers \(Y\) and \(-c\).
Thus equations~\eqref{eq:pure}--\eqref{eq:mixed} recover Long's moments
\[
 \int_{SU(2)}((1+c)(ad+b))^m\,dg=0,\qquad
 \int_{SU(2)}(-c)((1+c)(ad+b))^m\,dg
 =\frac{(-1)^{m-1}}{m+1}.
\]

Under the Müger--Tuset integration substitution used by Long,
\[
 (a,b,c,d)
 =\bigl((1-x)z_2,xz_1,-z_1^{-1},z_2^{-1}\bigr),
\]
the same seed becomes
\[
 (1-z_1^{-1})\bigl((1-x)+xz_1\bigr),
\]
which is Long's three-term counterexample to \(xz(1,1)\).
Accordingly, the seed and its \(xz\)- and \(SU(2)\)-interpretations are
due to Long.  The contribution here is the degree-two one-pair
bihomogenization that produces the four-term counterexample to the Special
Image Conjecture in three pairs.

\section{The two-pair boundary}\label{sec:two-pair}

The four-term construction gives failure with three pairs. The number of
pairs can be reduced at the cost of a larger polynomial. We include the
argument so that the dimension bound does not depend on an unavailable
extension of the three-pair proof.

In \(R_2\), put
\[
 R=\zeta_1z_1+\zeta_2z_2,\quad Z=\zeta_1z_2,\quad
 W=2\zeta_2z_1,\quad T=\zeta_1z_1-\zeta_2z_2.
\]
Thus \(T^2=R^2-2ZW\). Define
\[
 F=(R+Z)\left(R^2W-\tfrac12(2R+Z)T^2\right),
 \qquad Q=Z.
\]
The two bidegrees are \((4,4)\) and \((1,1)\), respectively.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:two-pair}
For every integer \(m\geq1\),
\[
 \Eop_2(F^m)=0,\qquad
 \Eop_2(QF^m)=\frac{(4m+2)!\,m!}{(2m+1)!!}\ne0.
\]
Consequently the minimum failing number of contraction pairs is two.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We calculate contraction by a beta integral. For every polynomial \(H\)
bihomogeneous of bidegree \((n,n)\), direct monomial evaluation gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beta-contraction}
 \Eop_2(H)=(n+1)!\operatorname{CT}_u
  \int_0^1H\left(1,u,s,\frac{1-s}{u}\right)\,ds.
\end{equation}
Indeed, the constant term of
\(\zeta_1^a\zeta_2^{n-a}z_1^bz_2^{n-b}\) vanishes unless \(a=b\).
In that case the beta integral is
\(a!(n-a)!/(n+1)!\), giving exactly the contraction.
Integration here is a rational polynomial functional, so the formula
also holds as a formal coefficient identity.

Under this substitution, write \(v=2s-1\) and
\(x=(1-v)/(2u)\). Then
\[
 R=1,\quad T=v,\quad Z=x,\quad ZW=(1-v^2)/2,
\]
and the polynomial \(F\) becomes
\[
 p(x,v)=\frac{1+x}{2x}\left(1-v^2(1+x)^2\right).
\]
The change \(x=(1-v)/(2u)\), made first over \(\mathbb Q(v)\),
preserves constant terms: it reverses each exponent and multiplies its
coefficient by a nonzero scalar, so exponent zero is unchanged. After
simplification, \(p\) is Laurent in \(x\) and polynomial in \(v\).
It is even in \(v\); hence integration over \(-1\leq v\leq1\), with
measure \(dv/2\), equals integration over \(0\leq v\leq1\).

For \(m\geq1\), define the polynomial
\[
 J_m(X)=\int_0^X(1-v^2)^m\,dv,\qquad
 H_m(X)=X^{m-1}J_m(X).
\]
Substitution \(v=sX\) also gives
\(H_m(X)=X^m\int_0^1(1-s^2X^2)^m\,ds\).
The normalized pure and mixed contractions in
\eqref{eq:beta-contraction} are therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:two-coefficients}
 2^{-m}[x^m]H_m(1+x),\qquad
 2^{-m}[x^{m-1}]H_m(1+x),
\end{equation}
respectively.

The central observation is that \(J'_m(X)=(1-X^2)^m\) has a zero
of order \(m\) at \(X=1\). Thus
\(J_m(1+x)=J_m(1)+x^{m+1}B(x)\) for some polynomial \(B\).
Through degree \(m\), the polynomial \(H_m(1+x)\) consequently
agrees with \(J_m(1)(1+x)^{m-1}\). The first coefficient in
\eqref{eq:two-coefficients} is zero, and the second is
\(2^{-m}J_m(1)\). Finally,
\[
 J_m(1)=\int_0^1(1-v^2)^m\,dv
       =\frac{2^m m!}{(2m+1)!!}.
\]
Applying \eqref{eq:beta-contraction} with \(n=4m\) and \(n=4m+1\)
proves both identities. Lemma~\ref{lem:kernel} proves failure for two
pairs. For one pair, apply~\cite[Theorem 2.8]{EWZ2011} with the
\(\mathbb Q\)-algebra \(A=\mathbb C[\zeta_1]\) and
\(a=\zeta_1\). The element \(a\) is a non-zero-divisor and
\((a)\) is radical, as required.
\end{proof}

\section{Size, scope, and verification}

The coefficients of \(f\) lie in \(\{1,-1\}\).  With respect to
\((\tau,w,v)\)-degree and \((t,z,y)\)-degree, \(f\) has bidegree
\((2,2)\) and ordinary degree four; \(g=y\) has bidegree \((0,1)\).

The two-pair polynomial has sixteen expanded terms and total degree
eight. These constructions exhibit a trade-off between the number of
pairs and the size of a witness. They do not establish minimum support
size or degree among all counterexamples in either dimension. Neither
polynomial is a separated product of a dual polynomial and a coordinate
polynomial, so the constructions do not by themselves give a
constant-coefficient Generalized Vanishing Conjecture counterexample.

Both proofs establish identities for every positive exponent without
relying on a finite search. The accompanying exact sparse-arithmetic
checks provide regression tests: the three-pair contraction identities
are checked through \(m=10\), and the two binomial sums through
\(m=99\). These finite checks supplement the proofs; they cannot replace
the all-order cancellation arguments.
